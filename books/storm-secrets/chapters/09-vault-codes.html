Caesar's secret letters

More than 2,000 years ago, the Roman general Julius Caesar needed to send orders that enemies couldn't read if they intercepted them. According to the Roman writer Suetonius, Caesar used a simple trick: he swapped every letter for the letter three places later in the alphabet. A became D, B became E, C became F, and so on. At the end of the alphabet you wrap back round to the start, so X becomes A, Y becomes B and Z becomes C.

This kind of secret writing is called a cipher, and this one is still known as the Caesar cipher. To encrypt a message, you shift forward. To decrypt it, you shift back.

The Caesar wheel
Outer ring: your message.
Inner ring: the secret letter.

To encrypt, go from outside to inside.
To decrypt, go from inside to outside.

Example: CAT → FDW

Dots, dashes and the telegraph

In the 1830s and 1840s, the American inventors Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail worked on a machine that could send messages down a long electric wire: the telegraph. The wire could only carry one thing, a pulse of electricity, either short or long. So they turned every letter into a pattern of short signals (dots) and long signals (dashes). On 24 May 1844, Morse sent a message along a wire from Washington to Baltimore, more than 60 kilometres away: "What hath God wrought". Within a few years, telegraph wires stretched across whole countries.

Real or fake? SOS means "Save Our Souls"

The famous distress signal SOS is three dots, three dashes, three dots: · · · – – – · · ·. Fake! Lots of people think it stands for "Save Our Souls" or "Save Our Ship", but it doesn't stand for anything. It was chosen because it's easy to send and impossible to mistake, even by someone who's panicking.

The greatest code-breakers in history

During the Second World War, the German army, navy and air force sent their secret messages using the Enigma machine, which looked like a typewriter in a wooden box. Inside, spinning wheels scrambled every letter differently each time a key was pressed, and the settings changed every day. There were so many possible settings that checking them one by one would have taken far longer than a lifetime.

The first people to crack it were three young Polish mathematicians: Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski. By the end of 1932, Rejewski had worked out how the machine's wheels were wired, using maths. In July 1939, just weeks before the war began, the Poles shared everything they knew with British and French codebreakers.

In Britain, the codebreakers worked in secret at Bletchley Park, a country house north of London. Thousands of people worked there, and most of them were women. The mathematician Alan Turing and his colleague Gordon Welchman designed a huge code-cracking machine called the Bombe, partly based on a Polish machine called the bomba. Other brilliant codebreakers, like Joan Clarke, worked alongside them. Their work was kept secret for about 30 years after the war. Historians believe it helped shorten the war and saved many lives.
