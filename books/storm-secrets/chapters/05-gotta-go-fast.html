How fast is fast?

Speed is all about two things: how far you go, and how long it takes. If you know both, you can work out the speed with one simple rule:

speed = distance ÷ time

So if you walk 10 kilometres in 2 hours, your speed is 10 ÷ 2 = 5 kilometres per hour (km/h). Here's how some speedy things compare:

What is sound, anyway?

Sound is made of vibrations. When you clap, your hands squash the air between them, and that squash travels outwards like a ripple on a pond, bumping from air particle to air particle until it reaches your ear. On a normal day, that ripple travels at about 343 metres every second. That's about 1,235 km/h: more than ten times faster than a car on the motorway.

Light is much, much faster. That's why you see lightning before you hear the thunder, even though they happen at the same moment. The light reaches you almost instantly. The sound is still on its way.

Breaking the sound barrier

For a long time, nobody knew if a plane could fly faster than sound. As planes got close to that speed, the air piled up in front of them in a kind of invisible wall, and they shook violently. Pilots called it the sound barrier, and some engineers thought it could never be broken.

On 14 October 1947, an American test pilot named Chuck Yeager climbed into a bright orange rocket plane called the Bell X-1. He'd named it Glamorous Glennis, after his wife. The X-1 was too thirsty for fuel to take off by itself, so a huge bomber carried it up underneath and let it drop. Yeager fired the rockets, climbed to about 13,000 metres, and pushed past the speed of sound. People on the ground heard a sonic boom: the thunder-like bang made when something goes faster than its own sound waves.

True story: the broom that broke the sound barrier

Two nights before his record flight, Yeager fell off a horse and cracked two ribs. He was in so much pain that he couldn't pull the X-1's cockpit door shut. So his friend, the engineer Jack Ridley, cut off a piece of a broom handle and gave it to him to use as a lever. Fortnite's Witch Broom is cool, but it has never broken the sound barrier.

Speeds this fast are measured in Mach numbers, named after the Austrian scientist Ernst Mach, who studied shock waves in the 1800s. Mach 1 is the speed of sound. Mach 2 is twice the speed of sound. Yeager reached Mach 1.06: just a little bit supersonic.

Later, passengers could go supersonic too. Concorde, a sleek plane with a pointy nose, flew from 1976 to 2003 at about Mach 2. It could cross the Atlantic Ocean from London to New York in about three and a half hours, roughly half the time of a normal jet.
