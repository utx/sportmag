Real-life Sprite: the honeybee

Power: perfect building. Bees store honey in wax cells, and every single cell is a six-sided hexagon. Making wax takes bees loads of energy, so they can't waste it. Hexagons fit together with no gaps, and they need less wax for their walls than triangles or squares holding the same amount of honey. The most efficient shape possible! People suspected this for about 2,000 years, but nobody could prove it until a mathematician finally did in 1999. The bees knew all along.

Why bees build hexagons
Circles leave gaps. Hexagons share every wall.

Bonus power: dancing directions. When a bee finds great flowers, she flies home and does a "waggle dance" on the honeycomb. The direction she waggles tells the other bees which way to fly, compared to the sun, and the longer she waggles, the further away the flowers are. A scientist named Karl von Frisch worked this out and won a Nobel Prize for it in 1973.

Real-life Sprite: the vampire bat

Power: sharing dinner. The Vampire Sprite steals health from enemies. Real vampire bats, which live in Central and South America, really do drink blood, usually from sleeping cows and horses. But they're also some of the most generous animals scientists have ever studied. A bat that misses dinner a couple of nights in a row can starve. So when a bat comes home hungry, a roost-mate often shares some of its own meal (by bringing it back up: yes, really). And bats that have been helped are more likely to help back later. They reciprocate.

Answer: Quest 11.1

1⁄3 (one in three).

How to crack it: there are 6 Sprites, and 2 of them are Vampires. So the chance is 2 out of 6, which is 2⁄6. That's the same as 1⁄3: if you grabbed from this chest lots of times, you'd get a Vampire about once in every three tries.

Vault Quest 11

A worker honeybee makes only about 1⁄12 of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life. How many teaspoons of honey do 12 bees make in their lifetimes?

Turn the page for the answer and your vault letter.

Answer: Vault Quest 11

1 teaspoon. Each bee makes one twelfth, and twelve twelfths make one whole. So it takes twelve bees their whole lives to make one teaspoon of honey. Think about that next time you have honey on toast.

Geno's Worst Take

"Everything with wings can fly. Penguins have wings. So penguins can fly. And my cape looks a bit like wings, so I can probably fly too."

Geno's argument follows a pattern that looks logical. So why is it wrong?

Turn the page for the roast.

The roast

The very first part is false: not everything with wings can fly. Penguins, ostriches and emus all have wings and stay firmly on the ground. If an argument starts with something untrue, the ending can't be trusted, however logical it sounds. (And a cape is not wings, Geno.)
