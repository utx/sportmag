Inside a ghost’s brain

Fly over the north of the Island and you'll spot an island shaped like a hungry yellow circle with a slice missing, and a couple of little ghost-shaped islands down south. They're there because of Pac-Man, the famous 1980 arcade game (it belongs to another company, so no pictures). At night, robot ghosts roam around them. Beat one and you might win the Blinky Sprite, which hides you for a moment whenever you take damage.

In the original game, four ghosts chase you around a maze. They seem to have personalities: one's relentless, one's sneaky, one's unpredictable and one's a bit hopeless. But here's the secret: none of them can think. Each one just follows a simple rule. Learn the rules and you can beat them.

Four ghosts, four rules

GhostJapanese name means…Its rule
Blinky (red)"Chaser"Head for the exact square where the player is right now.
Pinky (pink)"Ambusher"Head for the square four spaces in front of the player, to cut them off.
Inky (blue)"Fickle" (moody)Use a tricky rule that depends on where both the player AND Blinky are. Hard to predict!
Clyde (orange)"Playing dumb"Chase the player when far away, but when he gets close, wander off to his corner.

Four tiny rules, and together they make a chase that feels clever and alive. A set of step-by-step rules like this is called an algorithm. The ghosts in Pac-Man, the robots guarding Override Consoles, the bots you meet in your first few matches: they're all algorithms.

Real or fake? The pizza story

The most famous story about Pac-Man is that its designer, Toru Iwatani, got the idea when he took a slice of pizza and looked at what was left. Half true! Iwatani himself has told the story, but in 1986 he admitted it was only "half true". The shape also came from rounding off the Japanese written symbol for "mouth", which is a square.

True story: the bug that was a real bug

On 9 September 1947, engineers working on an enormous early computer at Harvard University found something stuck inside it: a real moth. They taped it into their logbook with a note: "First actual case of bug being found." Engineers already called mistakes "bugs", but the moth made the joke famous. Fixing mistakes in a program is still called debugging.

Program the Robot Llama

Your turn to write an algorithm. The Robot Llama lives on this grid. Squares are named by letter, then number, so the bottom-left square is A1. It always starts on A1, facing north (up the page), and it understands just three commands: F (move forward one square), L (turn left) and R (turn right). Turning doesn't move it.

Answer: Quest 7.1

It ends on E6, one square past the Chug Jug. Delete the last F.

How to crack it: F F takes it from A1 up to A3. R turns it to face east. F F F F moves it along to E3. L turns it to face north again. Then F F F moves it to E4, E5 (the Chug Jug!) and E6. One step too many. Remove the final F and it stops right on the Chug Jug.
