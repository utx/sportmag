Chapter 5
The blocks that flattened a town

Down on the south coast of the Island, a quiet seaside town has been flattened. Sunken Shores is half underwater, and it's been squashed by gigantic falling blocks, each one made of four chunky squares, scattered around like a toddler's toy box.

Those blocks come from Tetris, one of the most famous video games ever made, and one of the "gaming legends" that inspired this season's Overrides. (Tetris belongs to another company, so we won't show its official pictures. But the shapes themselves? Anyone can draw those.)

True story: the game made of brackets

In June 1984, in Moscow, a computer scientist called Alexey Pajitnov invented a game about falling blocks. The computer he was using couldn't even draw pictures, only letters and symbols, so he built each block out of square brackets: [ ][ ][ ][ ]. He named it by mixing tetra (Greek for "four", because every block has four squares) with tennis, his favourite sport. People couldn't stop playing it, and it spread around the world. The twist? Because of the rules in his country at the time, Pajitnov didn't earn any money from his famous game for twelve whole years.

Meet the seven blocks

There are exactly seven ways to join four squares together edge to edge. Mathematicians call them tetrominoes:

The seven tetrominoes
Every one is made of exactly four squares
S and Z are mirror images of each other. So are J and L.

Look closely at S and Z. Hold S up to a mirror, and you see Z! They're mirror images. So are J and L. In Tetris you can turn a block around as much as you like, but you can never flip it over, so an S can never become a Z.

IRL Challenge: Draw all seven blocks on squared paper and cut them out. Can you fit them together to tessellate (no gaps, no overlaps) into a rectangle? Try using just four of them first: the I, the O, the L and the J make a neat 4 by 4 square.

Here's a game in progress. Which piece, A, B, C or D, would fill the gap perfectly? (You can turn it, but not flip it.) How many complete lines would it clear?

Quest 5.1: fill the gap

Turn the page for the answer.

Answer: Quest 5.1

Piece B, and it clears 3 lines.

How to crack it: the gap is one square wide in the bottom two rows, then two squares wide in the third row up, sticking out to the left. That's an L-shape, and piece B fits it exactly. Once it drops in, all three bottom rows are full, so they all disappear.

Vault Quest 5

A Tetris board is 10 squares wide. You want to clear exactly 6 complete lines, with no squares left over. How many blocks do you need? (Remember: every block is made of 4 squares.)

Turn the page for the answer and your vault letter.

Answer: Vault Quest 5

15 blocks.

How to crack it: 6 lines of 10 squares is 6 × 10 = 60 squares to fill. Each block fills 4 squares, so you need 60 ÷ 4 = 15 blocks.

Vault letter: O (the 15th letter of the alphabet). Write it in box 5.

The roast

Wrong again! S and Z are mirror images. You can turn an S around as many times as you like and it will never become a Z. The only way is to flip it over, and Tetris doesn't let you do that. To prove it, cut an S out of paper and try to match it to a Z without turning the paper over. You can't.
