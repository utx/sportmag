Builds that never break

In Build mode, you can throw up a wall in a split second. Fortnite gives you four building pieces: walls, floors, ramps and roofs (the pointy roof is called a cone). Every piece costs 10 materials, and you can build with wood, brick or metal. Wood goes up fastest but ends up weakest. Metal takes longest to build but ends up toughest. Pro builders can put up a whole tower in the time it takes you to say "wait, where did they go?"

Real engineers don't get to build in half a second. But they've spent thousands of years working out the same question you do in every build fight: how do you make something that won't fall down?

The secret of the triangle

Make a square out of four sticks, with a pin at each corner so the joints can turn. Push on one corner, and the square squashes flat into a diamond shape. Now make a triangle out of three sticks, and push. Nothing happens. A triangle can't change its shape unless one of its sides changes length. That makes it rigid, and that's why engineers love triangles.

Wobbly square, solid triangle
Push from the side and see what happens

Once you know the trick, you'll see triangles everywhere: in roof frames, cranes, electricity pylons, bike frames and the steel of big bridges. Engineers call a framework of triangles a truss.

IRL Challenge: Make a square and a triangle from paper strips (or straws), joined at the corners with split pins or pipe-cleaners. Push them. Then add one diagonal strip across the square and push again.

The pyramids and the arch

The oldest way to build something that stays up is to make it wide at the bottom and narrow at the top. About 4,500 years ago, the ancient Egyptians built the Great Pyramid of Giza for the pharaoh Khufu. It was about 146 metres tall, and it stayed the tallest human-made structure on Earth for more than 3,800 years, until a cathedral in Lincoln, England, got a giant spire in the 1300s.

The Romans had a cleverer trick: the arch. In an arch, every stone pushes against its neighbours, and the load gets passed around the curve and down into the ground. The wedge-shaped stone at the very top, the keystone, locks everything in place. Using arches and a new invention, concrete, the Romans built bridges, aqueducts to carry water, and the Pantheon in Rome. Its huge concrete dome was finished nearly 1,900 years ago, and it's still the biggest dome in the world made of concrete with no steel inside.

How an arch holds up a load

The Coathanger

Sydney's most famous structure is a giant steel arch. The Sydney Harbour Bridge opened on 19 March 1932, and Sydneysiders soon nicknamed it "the Coathanger". Its chief engineer was John Bradfield, and it was built by an English company called Dorman Long. Look closely at the arch and you'll see it's packed with triangles: it's one enormous truss.

Building it was a high-wire act. Workers built the two halves of the arch out from each shore at the same time, with neither half touching anything in the middle. Huge steel cables, anchored in tunnels in the rock behind each end, held the half-arches back so they didn't topple into the harbour. Slowly the two halves crept towards each other, and on 19 August 1930 they finally met in the middle. Altogether the bridge used about 52,800 tonnes of steel and about six million rivets.

True story: the man who cut the ribbon first

On opening day, a huge crowd watched as the Premier of New South Wales, Jack Lang, got ready to cut the ribbon. Suddenly a man in uniform rode forward on a horse and slashed the ribbon with his sword! He was Francis de Groot, who belonged to a political group that disliked the Premier. He was arrested and fined £5. The ribbon was tied back together, and the Premier cut it again.
