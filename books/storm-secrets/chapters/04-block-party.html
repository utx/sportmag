The game made of brackets

In June 1984, in Moscow, a computer scientist named Alexey Pajitnov was working at a computing centre run by the Soviet Academy of Sciences. Part of his job was trying out new computers. His hobby was puzzles, and his favourite was a game called pentominoes, where you fit shapes made of five squares into a box.

Pajitnov wondered: what if the shapes fell, and you had to fit them together as they came down? The computer he was using, an Electronika 60, couldn't even draw pictures. It could only show letters and symbols. So he built each block out of square brackets, like this: [ ][ ][ ][ ]. It didn't look like much, but people who tried it couldn't stop playing.

He named it by mixing two words: tetra, the Greek word for "four" (every piece is made of four squares), and tennis, his favourite sport. Copies spread from computer to computer around Moscow on floppy disks. Then it spread around the world, and in 1989 it came packed in with Nintendo's new Game Boy. It became one of the best-selling video games of all time.

True story: the inventor who didn't get paid

In the Soviet Union, people weren't allowed to own businesses, so the government claimed the rights to Tetris. Pajitnov earned almost nothing from his famous game for years. It wasn't until 1996, twelve years after he invented it, that he finally started getting paid for his work.

Meet the tetrominoes

A shape made of squares joined edge to edge is called a polyomino (the mathematician Solomon Golomb gave them that name in 1953). Two squares make a domino. Four squares make a tetromino, and there are exactly seven of them in Tetris:

The seven tetrominoes
Every one is made of exactly four squares
S and Z are mirror images of each other. So are J and L.

Look closely at S and Z. If you hold S up to a mirror, you see Z! The same goes for J and L. You can rotate a Tetris piece as much as you like, but you can never reflect it, so an S can never become a Z. Mathematicians say there are only five different tetrominoes if you're allowed to flip them over, but seven if you're not.

Add one more square and things get busier: there are 12 pentominoes (the shapes that inspired Pajitnov). With six squares there are 35 shapes, and the numbers keep exploding from there.

Why some shapes fit and some don't

When shapes cover a flat surface with no gaps and no overlaps, mathematicians say they tessellate. Tetrominoes are great at it. So are lots of shapes you see every day: brick walls, floor tiles, the hexagons on a soccer ball (almost) and, as you'll discover in chapter 10, the cells in a honeycomb.

But here's a strange fact. Of all the regular shapes (the ones with every side and every angle the same), only three can tessellate on their own: the equilateral triangle, the square and the regular hexagon. The secret is the angles. Where the corners of the tiles meet at a point, the angles must add up to exactly 360°, a full turn.
