The storm’s sneaky trick

The storm is coming. A new circle appears on your map, and you have to get inside it before the wall closes in. You've done this a thousand times. But have you ever noticed that the late game feels crowded way faster than it should? You're not imagining it. Every time the circle shrinks, you lose much more space than it looks like. The storm has a sneaky trick, and once you know it, you'll never get caught rotating late again.

The secret is hiding in the shape of the storm itself, and in a number so strange that people have spent more than 2,000 years trying to pin it down.

The storm’s secret shape

The three measurements of a circle

Wrap a piece of string around any circle, from a coin to a Ferris wheel, and measure it. Then measure straight across. The distance around is always a bit more than three times the distance across. Not exactly three. About 3.14. Always. For every circle in the universe.

That number is called pi (say it "pie"), and it's written with the Greek letter π. The trouble is, pi never ends. Its decimal places go on forever without repeating: 3.14159265358979… Computers have now worked out trillions of digits, though nobody needs that many. NASA's engineers say about 15 decimal places is plenty for steering spacecraft around the solar system.

IRL Challenge: find pi in your kitchen. Wrap string around a round tin, plate or cup, mark where it meets, and measure the string. Then measure across the middle. Divide the first number by the second (a grown-up's calculator is allowed here). How close to 3.14 did you get?

The man who trapped a circle

About 2,250 years ago, in the city of Syracuse on the island of Sicily, lived Archimedes, one of the greatest mathematicians who ever lived. He wanted to know pi as exactly as possible, but he had no calculator, no decimals, and not even a pencil (he probably drew in sand or dust).

His trick was brilliant. Measuring a curve is hard, but measuring straight lines is easy. So he drew a six-sided shape just inside a circle, and another just outside it. The circle's edge has to be longer than the inside shape's and shorter than the outside shape's. Pi was trapped between them!

Archimedes' trap
Shapes inside and outside the circle squeeze pi between them
Archimedes kept doubling the sides: 6, 12, 24, 48, and finally 96. Each time, the trap got tighter.

Then he doubled the number of sides to 12, then 24, then 48, and finally 96, doing all the sums by hand. With 96 sides, his shapes hugged the circle so tightly that he could say pi is bigger than 310⁄71 and smaller than 31⁄7. In decimals, that's somewhere between about 3.1408 and 3.1429. The real value, 3.1416, sits right in the middle. Not bad for someone working in the sand!

Real or fake? Two famous Archimedes stories

"Eureka!" The story goes that Archimedes was asked to check whether a king's crown was pure gold. While climbing into a bath, he noticed the water rising, realised he could measure the crown's volume the same way, and ran through the streets shouting "Eureka!" ("I've found it!"). The trouble is, the story was written down by a Roman, Vitruvius, about 200 years after Archimedes died, and historians aren't sure how much of it really happened.

"Don't disturb my circles!" When Roman soldiers captured Syracuse, Archimedes is said to have been so busy with a diagram that he told a soldier not to disturb his circles. Different ancient writers tell the story differently, so it's probably more legend than fact.

The storm’s sneaky trick

Now back to the storm. The space inside a circle is called its area, and there's a famous rule for it: area = π × radius × radius. Mathematicians write it as A = πr². The important part is that the radius gets multiplied by itself.

So what happens when the storm circle's radius is cut in half? You might think you lose half your space. But look:

Half as wide, a quarter of the space
Each little square is 1 unit by 1 unit

Halve the radius, and the area shrinks to a quarter. That's because 4 × 4 = 16, but 2 × 2 = 4, and 4 is a quarter of 16. Three-quarters of the safe space vanished, even though the circle only got "half as big". No wonder the late game feels so crowded!

Chug Jug tip: When the next circle appears, don't run around the edge of the storm. Cut straight across. The way around half a circle is about 1.57 times as long as the way straight across it (see Quest 2.5).
