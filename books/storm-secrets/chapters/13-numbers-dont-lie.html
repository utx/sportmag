How champions win

The biggest event in competitive Fortnite is the FNCS Global Championship. In 2026 it was played in duos (pairs) on 26 and 27 September, with 50 teams from around the world competing for a $2 million prize pool over 12 matches. After all that, the winners finished just two points ahead of second place.

So how did they do it? The answer is hiding in the results table, and most people read straight past it. (Later in this chapter you'll meet two people who used numbers like these to save thousands of lives.)

How to lie with a chart

Charts are brilliant for showing data at a glance. But a chart can also trick you, even when every number on it is true. The most common trick is to start the side of the chart (the axis) at a number bigger than zero. Look at these two charts. They show exactly the same numbers:

Same numbers, different story
First place 525 points, second place 523 points

On the sneaky chart, first place looks like it crushed second place. On the honest chart, you can see the truth: it was incredibly close. Whenever you see a chart, check where the axis starts!

The nurse who fought with diagrams

In 1854, Britain was fighting the Crimean War. Florence Nightingale, a nurse, took a team of nurses to the British army hospital at Scutari (in today's Istanbul, in Turkey). What she found was horrifying. The hospital was dirty and overcrowded, and soldiers were dying in huge numbers. Most of them weren't dying from their battle wounds. They were dying from diseases that spread in dirty conditions, and those deaths could have been prevented.

Soldiers called her "the Lady with the Lamp", because she walked the wards at night. But her greatest weapon turned out to be statistics. Back in Britain, she carefully studied the numbers of deaths and their causes, and in 1858 she published her findings using clever diagrams. Her most famous one, now called the rose diagram, uses wedges of different sizes for each month. The huge wedges for deaths from disease made the problem impossible to ignore. Her work helped persuade the government to make army hospitals cleaner and safer. That same year, she became the first woman elected to the Statistical Society of London, which is now the Royal Statistical Society.

True story: the doctor and the deadly pump

In the same year, 1854, a terrible disease called cholera broke out in Soho, in London. Most people thought diseases spread through "bad air". A doctor named John Snow wasn't so sure. He marked every death on a map of the streets and found that the deaths were clustered around one water pump, on Broad Street. He persuaded the local officials to take the pump's handle off so nobody could use it. The outbreak was already slowing by then, but Snow's map helped prove that cholera spreads through dirty water, and it changed how people fight disease. His data told a story that nobody could argue with.
