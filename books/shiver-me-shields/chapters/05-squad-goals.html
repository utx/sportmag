Chapter 5
Squad goals

A ship needs a crew. One sailor can't raise the anchor, set the sails, steer and keep watch all at once, but a good crew, working together, can cross an ocean. Fortnite squads are just the same. The last tip in Jake's video, and two bonus tips at the very end, are all about the moments when you need your crew, and when your crew needs you.

Tip 15: Reboot vans and reboot cards

When a teammate is eliminated, they drop a reboot card. Carry their card to a Reboot Van and you can bring them back into the match. That much hasn't changed. But this season, Jake says, two things work differently, and knowing them could save your squad.

A reboot card: your teammate's ticket back into the match. (Image: Epic Games / Fortnite)

The vans can drive, and you can revive yourself in the back. Drivable Reboot Vans are back this season, and Jake points out something special about them: "you can revive yourself in the back of these reboot vans." So if you're knocked down (lying on the ground, crawling, waiting for a teammate to help you up) and your squad can't reach you, don't just lie there. Crawl towards the van! If your teammates are busy fighting and keeping the enemy distracted, you might be able to sneak into the van and revive yourself. Jake describes it as a win-win: either you get yourself back up, or the enemy turns to chase you, which takes the pressure off your teammates.

The card comes back by itself. Last season, if your teammate's card expired before anyone picked it up, you could buy it back with gold. With gold gone, that's impossible. Instead, Jake explains, "if the card expires, you basically just get it and then you can reboot them normally." In other words: if nobody can collect your teammate's card in time, it simply turns up in your inventory once the timer runs out. Jake says the wait is shorter than it used to be, about a minute, but adds that it's still a long time to wait in the middle of a battle. Grabbing the card yourself is always faster. Waiting is the back-up plan.

Why it works. A squad that knows the rules gets its players back faster, and a squad with four players beats a squad with three. Remember the golden rules from the start of the book? Escape and heal. In a squad there's a third: never give up on a teammate.

Ever wondered where the word "reboot" comes from? It's a computer word, and it has a wonderfully strange history. There's an old English saying about someone "pulling themselves up by their own bootstraps", meaning lifting yourself off the ground by tugging on the loops at the back of your boots. It's impossible, of course, which is the joke. But early computer engineers loved the phrase, because starting a computer felt a bit like that: a tiny program has to run first and load a bigger program, which loads an even bigger one, until the whole machine has, in a way, lifted itself up. They called it bootstrapping, then booting, and starting it again is rebooting. So every time you reboot a teammate, you're using a word that began as a joke about boots.

Try it: Next time you play in a squad, agree a plan before you land: "If one of us goes down, where's the nearest van?" Find the Reboot Vans on your map early, just in case.

Bonus tip: watch the Captain

Jake's next tip happens before you've even landed. Remember the Going Merry, the ship that carries every player over the island? In every match, one player is chosen as the Captain, and the Captain can steer the ship, changing its route while you're still on board.

All aboard the Going Merry. Somebody on board is holding the wheel. (Frame: TG Plays)

What Jake says. In the old days, you'd look at the Battle Bus's route, pick your spot and plan your jump. Now, he warns, "the route can be changing constantly in real time." So you need to keep watching what the Captain is doing, and be ready to change your plan. If you'd planned to drop at the very end of the route, and the Captain swings the ship the other way, your perfect landing spot might suddenly be miles away.

Why it works. Good players are adaptable. They make a plan, but they keep checking whether the plan still makes sense, and they change it the moment it doesn't. That's a skill worth far more than any single landing spot.

Real ships' captains had to keep track of where they were going too, long before GPS or satellites. One of their cleverest tools was a chip log: a flat piece of wood tied to a long rope. A sailor threw the wood overboard, where it floated and stayed still while the ship sailed on, pulling the rope out behind it. Along the rope, knots were tied at equal distances, about 14 metres apart. While the rope ran out, a second sailor watched a little sand-timer that took 28 seconds to empty, and when the sand ran out, they counted how many knots had slipped over the side. If five knots went out, the ship was travelling at five "knots". That's why ships and planes still measure their speed in knots today! And the speeds, along with the ship's position, the weather and anything else important, were written down every day in a book named after that floating piece of wood: the ship's log. When you write in your Ship's Log at the end of each chapter, that's where the name comes from.

Try it: In your next match, wait until halfway along the Going Merry's route, then check: has the route changed since the start? Did the Captain do anything strange? Make a note in your Ship's Log.

Bonus tip: silence the sea shanties

Jake's final tip is a surprisingly popular one. The Going Merry plays pirate music as you fly over the island, and Jake says he's had a lot of comments from viewers who don't like it. His solution: go into the Audio settings and find the setting about licensed audio, which stops the game from playing copyrighted music. (Jake got it the wrong way round at first, then corrected himself: you turn it on to hide the music.) The ship's music disappears, and you can fly in peace.

Chug Jug check: ❓ This is Jake's tip, and the exact name of the setting may vary. If you can't find it, ask a grown-up to help you look through the Audio settings together.

Mind you, we'd be sorry to see the music go, because songs at sea have a long and fascinating history. Sailors on old sailing ships sang special work songs called sea shanties. They weren't just for fun. Hauling up a heavy anchor or a huge wet sail needed a whole crew pulling at exactly the same moment, and a shanty kept everyone in time. One sailor, the shantyman, sang a line on his own, and the whole crew answered together, heaving on the rope on the strong beat. It's called call and response, and it turns a crowd of tired sailors into one big, rhythmic machine.

The most famous shanty of recent years has a surprising link to Sydney. In late 2020, a young Scottish postman called Nathan Evans posted a video of himself singing an old sea song called "Soon May the Wellerman Come" on TikTok. It went viral: millions of people joined in, adding harmonies, instruments and even bass lines, and within weeks a version of his song was number one in the British charts. He gave up delivering letters to become a singer.

But who was the Wellerman? The song comes from New Zealand, from the days of whaling. In the 1830s, three brothers called Weller, who had moved to Sydney, started a whaling station in southern New Zealand. Their supply ships brought food and goods to the whalers, and those ships, and the men on them, were called the "Wellermen". In the song, the hungry whalers are waiting for the Wellerman to arrive with "sugar and tea and rum". (Folk-song experts like to point out that it's really a sea ballad, a story song, rather than a true working shanty. But everybody calls it a shanty anyway.) A song that sailed from Sydney to New Zealand nearly two hundred years ago ended up on millions of phones. Not bad for a work song.

Do the maths

1. A sailor throws the chip log overboard and counts 7 knots slipping out before the sand-timer runs out. How fast is the ship going?

2. The knots are about 14 metres apart. Roughly how many metres of rope ran out in question 1?

3. Jake says an expired reboot card comes back after about a minute. If you knock out an enemy and they need 30 seconds to escape, would waiting for the card give your teammate time to help you chase? Explain your answer.

Answers
1: 7 knots
2: 7 × 14 = 98 metres (about 100)
3: No! 60 seconds is twice as long as 30. Grab the card yourself.

Chapter 5 in one breath

Never give up on a teammate. Crawl to a Reboot Van and revive yourself if nobody can reach you. Grab reboot cards quickly, but remember an expired card comes back on its own. Watch the Captain's route before you jump, and change your plan when it changes. And if the sea shanties drive you mad, you can turn them off (but now you know where they came from).
