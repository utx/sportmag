Football Brain
Game smarts

Watch a really good player and you'll notice something strange: they never seem to be in a hurry. The ball arrives, and they already know what to do with it. That isn't magic. It's football IQ, the thinking part of the game, and it's one of the four things the JDL measures ("mental engagement"). The great news is that, just like ball skills, it can be trained.

Scan, scan, scan

The single most useful football-brain habit is scanning: looking around, especially over your shoulder, before the ball comes to you. Each quick look is like taking a photo of the pitch. Where are my team-mates? Where are the opponents? Where's the space? When the ball arrives, you're not guessing. You're choosing.

Try to take a look just before a team-mate passes to you, and again as the ball is travelling. If you know there's no-one behind you, you can turn. If there's a defender right there, you can play it back first time or move the ball away from them with your first touch.

Try this: the shoulder-check game

With a friend: one of you passes, the other receives. Just before passing, the passer holds up some fingers behind the receiver. The receiver has to check over their shoulder, shout the number, then control the ball. Swap after ten goes.

Make the pitch big, then small

Here's a rule good teams follow. When your team has the ball, make the pitch big: spread out wide and long, so the other team has to cover lots of ground and there's space to pass into. When the other team has the ball, make the pitch small: squeeze together, get close to the ball and close down the space they want to use.

Everyone around the ball means nobody is in space. JDL football teaches you to spread out. Photo: Edward N. Johnson, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

The moment the ball changes teams is called a transition, and it's when the most goals happen in junior football. The fastest-thinking team in those few seconds usually wins the moment. Don't stop to complain when you lose the ball. Win it back!

Playing out from the back

Because of the retreat line, every JDL team gets a chance to build attacks calmly from its own goal. Football NSW recommends a 1-3-2-3 or 1-3-3-2 shape for Under 10s (the "1" is the goalkeeper). Here's how a 1-3-2-3 might spread out when your keeper has the ball:

Notice how far apart everyone is. The wide defenders go almost to the touchlines. The midfielders stand in the gaps between opponents, not behind them. The forwards stay high and wide, which pushes the other team back. Every player is asking a question: "Can the ball get to me from here?" If the answer is no, move.

Your coach may play a different shape, and you'll probably play lots of positions. That's on purpose: a defender who has played up front knows what attackers hate. The more positions you try, the bigger your football brain grows.

Talk

Good teams are noisy, in a helpful way. A few short words can save a team-mate's possession:

  • "Time!": nobody is near you, take a touch.
  • "Man on!" (or "Player on!"): someone's coming to tackle you.
  • "Turn!": there's space behind you, spin and go.
  • "Here!" or your name: I'm free, pass to me.
  • "Squeeze!": we've lost it, everyone get closer.

Watch like a coach

You can train your football brain on the couch. Next time you watch a professional match, A-League, Matildas or Premier League, try this: for five minutes, don't watch the ball. Pick one player in your position and watch only them. When do they scan? Where do they run when their team has the ball? Where do they go when it's lost? Better still, go to a live game, where you can see the whole pitch at once.

Coach's corner

Before the ball comes, ask yourself: "What will I do if it comes to me?" Having a plan, and a back-up plan, is half of football IQ.
