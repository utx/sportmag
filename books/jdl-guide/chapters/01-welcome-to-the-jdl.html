Welcome to the JDL
The league

Picture a Saturday morning in March. Across Sydney, hundreds of nine- and ten-year-olds are pulling on their club shirts for a game that looks a lot like football, with a few clever differences. By lunchtime, nobody will be able to look up the scores online, because they won't be there. That's not a mistake. It's the whole idea.

What is the JDL?

The Junior Development League is run by Football NSW, the organisation in charge of football across most of New South Wales. It's a league for talented young players, organised through clubs that hold a JDL licence. There are two parts:

  • Mixed JDL, for boys and girls in the Under 9, Under 10, Under 11 and Under 12 age groups. "Mixed" means girls and boys can play in the same team: each club picks at least four girls for every age group.
  • Girls JDL, for girls in the Under 10, Under 11, Under 12 and Under 13 age groups.

In 2026 there were 45 clubs in the Mixed JDL, and 27 Girls JDL programs. Each Mixed JDL club has two teams in every age group, with up to 12 players in each team.

Where it came from

The JDL is new, but the idea behind it isn't. In August 2011, Football NSW announced a Skill Acquisition Program, or SAP, which started in 2012. It was part of a plan across Australia to find talented 9 to 13 year olds and teach them the game properly, using the same national curriculum everywhere. A Girls SAP followed in 2016.

In 2024, Football NSW started checking every club's program against a set of standards, and in 2025 it gave the whole thing a new name: the Junior Development Leagues. If you hear a grown-up call it "SAP", now you know why. They're talking about the same thing.

Why nobody keeps score

Here's the part that surprises new players. In the JDL, match results aren't published. There's no ladder, no points table and no championship. Of course, you'll know the score on the day. Somebody always does. But it won't go on a website or a scoreboard, and it won't decide anything.

Football NSW explains it like this: JDL clubs should be thought of a bit like schools, where long-term learning is what matters. Nobody gives your class a ladder position for its spelling tests; the point is that every student gets better. When there's no ladder, a coach can let you try a new skill, play a new position, and make mistakes while you learn.

The four core skills

The JDL follows Football Australia's national curriculum, which puts young players aged about 9 to 13 in the Skill Acquisition Phase. That's the time of life when your brain and body are brilliant at learning new movements, so it's the time to stack up skills. The curriculum names four core skills:

  1. 1v1: beating a defender, and stopping an attacker.
  2. First touch: controlling the ball the moment it arrives.
  3. Running with the ball: moving fast with the ball under control.
  4. Passing and striking: from short passes to shots on goal.

Chapter 5 is all about them. For now, just remember: these four skills are what your coaches are building, every session.

How clubs get a JDL team

Not every football club can enter the JDL. A club needs a licence, and every season Football NSW's technical staff visit to watch training sessions and matches. They also check the club's plans, its coaches' qualifications and how many players and coaches stay from year to year. Then they give the club a score and a rating badge:

  • Gold, the top rating;
  • Silver, which Football NSW wants every club to reach;
  • Bronze; and
  • Development Committed, for clubs Football NSW is helping to build up their program.

Brand-new JDL clubs don't have a rating yet. For 2026, only one Mixed JDL club in the whole state, NWS Spirit, was rated Gold. The badges in Chapter 4 come from these official lists.

Clubs are also organised into zones: North, East, West and South for the Sydney area, plus regional zones. Normally, a club can only sign players who live in the same zone as its home ground. That's why your JDL team-mates mostly come from your part of Sydney.

Where the JDL can lead

The JDL is the first step on a ladder. After the Under 12s (or Under 13s for girls), players can move into Football NSW's Youth Leagues, which run from Under 13 to Under 18. Above those are the senior leagues, the National Premier Leagues (NPL), and above those the A-League, overseas clubs and the national teams: the Matildas and the Socceroos.
