Mindset
The inside game

Every footballer, from the Under 9s to the World Cup, makes mistakes. Passes go astray. Shots fly over the bar. Keepers let in goals they should have saved. The difference between players who get better and players who give up isn't how many mistakes they make. It's what they do next.

The power of "yet"

A psychologist at Stanford University named Carol Dweck spent years studying how people learn. She noticed two ways of thinking. People with a fixed mindset believe talent is something you either have or you don't: "I'm just bad with my left foot." People with a growth mindset believe abilities grow with practice and effort: "I'm not good with my left foot yet."

That little word, yet, turns "I can't" into "I'm learning". The JDL is built for growth-mindset players: no ladder, just you getting better week after week.

Mistakes are information

When something goes wrong, your brain wants to feel embarrassed. Instead, try being curious. Why did that pass get cut out? Was it too slow? Did I look up first? Each mistake tells you something to practise. In the JDL, coaches actually want you to try difficult things, like a turn in tight space, a pass through a gap, or a dribble at a defender, because that's how you learn. A player who never makes a mistake is a player who never tries anything new.

The 3-second reset
For after a mistake
1
Breathe
One big breath: in through your nose, out through your mouth.
2
Brush it off
A small action, like wiping your hands on your shorts. It's gone.
3
Back in
Say your cue word ("Next ball!") and get back in position.

Practise the reset at training, so it's automatic on game day. The fastest way to make a second mistake is to keep thinking about the first one.

Nerves are normal

Butterflies before a trial or a big game? Good. That fluttery feeling is your body getting ready: your heart beats faster to send more energy to your muscles. Nervous and excited feel almost exactly the same inside. Try telling yourself "I'm excited" instead of "I'm nervous". Then focus on your first job: your first touch, your first pass, your first tackle. Once the game starts, the butterflies usually fly away.

Control the controllables

You can't control the weather, the Game Leader's decisions, how good the other team is, or whether your coach puts you on the bench for the second half. You can control:

  • your effort, every single minute;
  • your attitude, including your body language when things go wrong;
  • your preparation: sleep, food, water, a packed bag; and
  • how you treat other people.

Spend your energy on those four, and let the rest go.

Compare with yesterday's you

Someone in your squad will always be faster, taller or better at juggling, especially at your age, when kids grow at very different speeds. The only fair comparison is with yourself: am I better than I was last month?

Keep a simple training diary: one thing that went well, one thing to work on, and one thing you learned. After a few weeks, read it back. You'll see your own resilience on the page.

Being subbed, and missing out

In the JDL, players rotate on and off all the time, and Football NSW wants everyone to get fair playing time. Being subbed off doesn't mean you played badly. Use the bench time well: watch the game, notice where the space is, and be ready to go back on with a plan.

And if one day you don't get picked for a team you wanted, you're in famous company. In Chapter 11, you'll meet a Matilda who didn't play for Australia until she was nearly 24, and a Socceroo who went from a small club in Cairns to the World Cup qualifiers. They kept going. You can too.

Coach's corner

"Next ball" are the two most useful words in football. Whatever just happened, good or bad, the next ball is the one that matters.
