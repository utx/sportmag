Team Talk
Coaches and team-mates

Football is a team game played by individuals. The JDL focuses on making you a better player, but you'll do that inside a squad of about a dozen kids, with coaches, a Game Leader, an opposition, and a sideline full of parents. Getting on well with all of them is a skill too, and coaches notice it just as much as a good first touch.

Be easy to coach

Your coach has to teach lots of players at once, so the players who learn fastest are the ones who make it easy:

  • When the coach talks, the ball stops. Put your foot on it, look at the coach, and listen. It sounds simple, but it's the number-one habit of coachable players.
  • Try it their way. Even if you'd normally do something differently, give the coach's idea a proper go first.
  • Ask questions. "Where should I be when the ball's on the other wing?" is a brilliant question. Coaches love players who want to understand.
  • Take feedback as a gift. When a coach corrects you, they're investing in you. Say "Thanks, coach" and try again. No sulking, no excuses.
  • Be on time, with your boots, shin guards and water. Being ready shows respect.

Be a great team-mate

The best team-mates make everyone around them better. In the JDL, that matters even more, because the squad is your learning group: the better everyone gets, the better your training sessions become.

Mixed JDL squads have girls and boys training and playing together. Treat every team-mate as exactly that: a team-mate. The girls in your squad were picked for the same reason you were.

Respect the Game Leader and the opposition

The Game Leader is often a young referee who's learning too. They'll get some decisions wrong, just as players get some passes wrong. Accept the decision and get on with the game. Captains can ask a question politely; nobody else needs to say a thing. At the end, shake hands with the other team and thank the Game Leader. Clap good play by the opposition, too. In the JDL, everyone is encouraged to cheer skilful football, whichever shirt it's in.

Your family on the sideline

Football NSW asks spectators to cheer good play from both teams and not to coach from the sideline. That's actually good for you: during a game, you should be listening to your coach and your team-mates, not to five different voices shouting different instructions. If a grown-up in your family gets a bit loud, it's fine to talk to them about it after the game. You could even show them this page.

Leading without the armband

You don't need to be captain to be a leader. Leaders help pack up the cones, say hello to the new kid, and keep encouraging when the team is losing. Even the newest player in the squad can do that from the very first session.

Coach's corner

At the end of each training session, say "thanks, coach". Coaches remember players who do.
