The U10 Rulebook
The rules

Under 10 JDL football looks like the game you watch on TV, shrunk down to the right size for you. There are fewer players, a smaller ball, smaller goals and a smaller pitch. Every one of those changes has a reason: to give each player more touches, more decisions and more time on the ball. In a full-sized eleven-a-side game, a nine-year-old might touch the ball a handful of times. In a JDL game, you're in the action all morning.

These are the Under 10 rules from Football NSW's 2026 JDL documents. The same format is used by the Mixed Under 10s and 11s and the Girls Under 10s and 11s.

The pitch

A U10 pitch is a bit more than half the length of a full-sized one, and is usually marked out with cones or painted lines on part of a big field. The goals are portable, 5 metres wide and 2 metres high, and must be pegged or weighed down so they can't tip over.

The retreat line: the rule that changes everything

Look at the dashed line of cones 15 metres out from each goal. That's the retreat line (Football NSW also calls it the exclusion zone). It's the most important rule in JDL football, so it's worth understanding properly.

When the goalkeeper has the ball in their hands, or when there's a goal kick, the other team must retreat behind that line until the ball is in play. That means the defending team gets a calm, safe space to start an attack with short passes, instead of the goalkeeper booting the ball as far as possible and hoping.

Why does Football NSW want that? Because in the real game, the best teams in the world build their attacks from the back with clever passing. You can't learn to do that if a pack of opponents is breathing down your neck every time the keeper picks the ball up. The retreat line gives your team time to practise playing out, and it gives you, the defenders and midfielders, the job of finding space and asking for the ball. In the JDL, you will be expected to want the ball near your own goal. That takes composure, and composure takes practice.

Coach's corner

When your keeper has the ball, don't stand still. Spread out wide, check over your shoulder, and show you're free. The retreat line has given you time: use it.

The goalkeeper

JDL goalkeepers can use their hands anywhere inside their own penalty area, like keepers everywhere. The big difference comes when they restart play.

A keeper can still play a long pass, as long as the ball is placed on the ground first. The idea isn't to ban long passes. It's to ban aimless long kicks. And if a team-mate deliberately passes back to the keeper, the keeper must not pick it up. If they do, the other team gets a free kick from the 15 m line.

Restarts

  • Kick-off: from the centre spot, with everybody in their own half and opponents at least 5 metres away. The ball has to touch a team-mate before a goal can be scored.
  • Goal kick: taken from 1 to 2 metres in front of the goal line, inside the penalty area. The other team must be behind the retreat line until the ball is kicked. Then they can come in and challenge for it.
  • Throw-in: face the pitch, keep part of each foot on the ground on or behind the line, use both hands and throw from behind and over your head. Opponents stand 5 metres away. You can't score straight from a throw-in, and you can't touch it again until another player has. In the JDL, the closest player takes the throw, so nobody wastes time arguing about whose turn it is.
  • Corner: placed in the corner arc, with opponents 5 metres away. You can score straight from a corner.

Game Leaders often use a quick "Ready, set, go!" to get restarts moving. JDL football is meant to flow.

Free kicks and penalties

Free kicks are given for fouls, the same ones you'd see in any game: tripping, pushing, holding, kicking, charging, jumping at an opponent, tackling from behind, playing dangerously, blocking a player's path, deliberate handball and so on. Where the foul happens decides what kind of free kick it is.

What kind of free kick?
The attacking half · not to scale

Outside the 15-metre zone, free kicks are indirect: a goal only counts if the ball touches another player first. Football NSW wants teams to take these short and quickly, not lump them into the box. Inside the 15-metre zone, free kicks are direct, so you can shoot straight away. That's on purpose: it gives JDL players a chance to practise free-kick technique. A deliberate handball or a foul by a defender inside their own penalty area gives the attacking team a penalty, taken from 8 metres with only the goalkeeper in the way.

Offside, JDL-style

Offside does exist in the Under 10s, but it's used gently. An attacker is only called offside when they're clearly beyond the second-last defender, by about 2 or 3 metres or more, at the moment the ball is passed, and they're involved in the play. If it's close, under a metre, the Game Leader lets play go on.

Offside, JDL-style
Measured when the ball is passed

The point of the rule isn't to catch you out. It's to stop "goal hanging", where a player waits up near the other team's goal and never helps. In the JDL, everybody attacks and everybody defends.

The Game Leader

JDL games aren't controlled by a strict referee. They're run by a Game Leader: it might be a young referee, a club official, an older teenager or a parent. Their job is to keep the game flowing, help with restarts, and explain decisions, so if they give a free kick, they'll often tell you why. Game Leaders praise good play by both teams. Think of them as half referee, half teacher. Football NSW's Game Leader handbook lists four things a Game Leader should do: let the game flow, encourage, use "Ready, set, go!", and explain to educate.

There are usually no yellow or red cards. If someone commits a really serious foul, the player can be taken off for five minutes, or for the rest of the game, and the coach deals with it.

Subs, safety and the sideline

  • Subs can come on and off at any time, even while the ball is in play, as long as the player coming off is off the pitch first. Football NSW says equal playing time should be a priority: "Kids don't register to watch football – they just want to play!"
  • Shin guards must be worn by every player, every game. No exceptions.
  • Concussion: any player who might have a head injury must be checked by a doctor before playing again. The rule is simple: "If in doubt, sit it out."
  • Spectators are asked to cheer good play from both teams, and not to coach from the sideline or shout at mistakes. Each home club has jacketed marshals at every game to keep the sideline friendly.

Try this

Before your first game, ask a grown-up to quiz you: What's the retreat line for? What happens if the keeper drop kicks it? Where can you shoot straight from a free kick? If you can answer all three, you know more about JDL rules than most of the parents on the sideline.
