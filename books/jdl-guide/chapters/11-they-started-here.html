They Started Here Too
Inspiration

Every international footballer was once a nervous kid at their first training session with a new club. Here are six Australian stars with links to the clubs in this book. Some started young, some started late. Some came from big cities, some from country towns, and one arrived in Sydney as a six-year-old refugee. What they all share is that they kept going.

Johnny Warren: Captain Socceroo

Johnny Warren in 1968. Photo: W. Brindle, Australian News and Information Bureau, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Johnny Warren grew up in Botany, in Sydney's south-east, and played junior football for local teams Botany Methodists and Earlwood Wanderers. At just 15, in 1959, he joined Canterbury-Marrickville, today's Canterbury Bankstown FC. He started in the club's third-grade team and was in first grade by the end of the year.

He went on to play 42 games for Australia, captain the team 24 times, and play at the country's first World Cup in 1974. Later, on television, he spent years telling Australia that football could be one of its great sports. His famous line, "I told you so", now appears on fans' banners whenever the Socceroos or Matildas do something special, and the A-League's player of the year award is the Johnny Warren Medal.

The lesson: believe in the game, and in yourself, even when other people don't yet.

Clare Wheeler: never too late to start

Clare Wheeler (Newcastle Jets) in a W-League match against Canberra United. Photo: LittleBlinky, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Clare Wheeler didn't start playing football until she was ten, at a local club in Newcastle called Adamstown Rosebud. Three years later she was picked for the state youth league, and at 15 she was playing in the W-League (now the A-League Women) for the Newcastle Jets.

She later earned a commerce degree from the University of Sydney, where she lived at St Andrew's College. Sydney University SFC lists her among its former NPL players. She made her Matildas debut in 2021, scored her first goal for Australia against the Philippines in 2023, went to the Paris 2024 Olympics, and plays in England for Everton.

The lesson: starting "late" at ten didn't stop her. What you do from today matters more than when you started.

Clare Hunt: the late bloomer

Clare Hunt on the ball for Canberra United, in the same W-League match against Newcastle Jets. Photo: LittleBlinky, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Clare Hunt grew up in Grenfell, a small country town in central-west NSW, far from the big-city clubs. She joined the Canberra United academy at 15, and then had to wait. She didn't play her first game for the Matildas until February 2023, just weeks before her 24th birthday.

A few months later, she was in Australia's squad for the 2023 Women's World Cup. Since then she has played for Paris Saint-Germain in France and Tottenham Hotspur in England. Her best friend is Clare Wheeler: they met at St Andrew's College at the University of Sydney, and Sydney University SFC counts both of them among its former players.

The lesson: everybody develops at a different speed. Keep improving, and your moment can still come.

Amy Sayer: two good feet

Amy Sayer playing for Australia against England in 2018. Photo: EL Loko, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Amy Sayer grew up in Sydney's north and started playing at Northbridge FC because her older sibling played there. From 2013 she was part of the Football NSW Institute's teams, and in 2016 and 2017 she played for Sydney University, coached by former Matilda Heather Garriock. She signed for Canberra United in the W-League at just 15.

She's known for her vision, her passing and her ability to score with either foot. She studied at Stanford University in the United States, and plays for the Matildas and for Malmö in Sweden.

The lesson: practise with your weaker foot until defenders can't tell which one is "weaker".

Miloš Degenek: from refugee to three World Cups

Miloš Degenek playing for Australia against the United States at the 2026 World Cup. Photo: Bryan Berlin, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Miloš Degenek was born in Croatia in 1994. When he was a baby, his family had to flee a war and lived as refugees in Belgrade. In 2000, when he was six, they moved to Sydney and settled in Campsie. He went to Westfields Sports High School and the Australian Institute of Sport, and at 18 he joined the youth team of Stuttgart, a big German club, far from home.

It wasn't easy. Injuries kept him out for most of a season. But he kept working, and made his debut for the Socceroos in 2016 against England. He went to the 2018 World Cup, played every one of Australia's four games at the 2022 World Cup, and was picked again for the 2026 World Cup.

The lesson: where you start doesn't decide where you finish.

Michael Thwaite: the boy from Cairns

Michael Thwaite playing for Western Sydney Wanderers in 2017. Photo: Efcso, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Michael Thwaite played all his junior football at Saints Soccer Club in Cairns, in far north Queensland. As a teenager in 2002 he moved to Sydney to go to university, on a scholarship to play for Sydney University. From there he went to Marconi in the old National Soccer League, and then all over the world: Romania, Poland, Norway and China.

He played for the Socceroos at the 2007 Asian Cup, was part of the squad that qualified for the 2014 World Cup, and played 200 A-League games. He also earned a university degree in human movement science and became a qualified coach.

The lesson: a small club and a long way from the big city is no barrier, if you're ready when the chance comes.

Word Watch

Debutnoun
A person's first appearance, like a player's first game for a team. Say it "day-byoo".
Refugeenoun
A person who has had to leave their home country to escape danger, such as war.
Scholarshipnoun
Money or a place given to a student because of their talent, to help pay for their education.
Late bloomernoun
Someone whose talent shows up later than other people's. Flowers that bloom late are just as beautiful.

Your turn

None of these six knew, at nine or ten, where football would take them. Neither do you. But they practised, listened, looked after their bodies, bounced back from bad days and loved the game. That's the whole secret. See you on the pitch.
