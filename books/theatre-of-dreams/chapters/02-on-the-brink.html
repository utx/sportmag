Chapter 2
On the Brink
Part Two: The Fergie Years
Old Trafford9 Dec
1989

Manchester United 1, Crystal Palace 2.

It's a grey December afternoon, and Old Trafford is grumbling. United have just lost at home again. They haven't won a league game for weeks. They're sinking towards the bottom half of the table, and the most famous club in England looks ordinary, which is the one thing its supporters can't stand.

Then, in the crowd, a bedsheet goes up. Somebody has painted a message on it in big black letters. It complains about three years of excuses, calls the team a rude word, and ends with two words aimed straight at the manager: TA-RA FERGIE.

"Ta-ra" is how people in Manchester say goodbye.

Alex Ferguson had been in charge for more than three years. He had spent millions on new players. He had not won a single trophy. And now the fans, the people whose opinion matters most at any football club, were telling him to leave. The newspapers were asking how long he had left. The answer, as it turned out, was twenty-three and a half more years. But nobody knew that in December 1989, least of all Ferguson.

The man from Govan

Ferguson grew up in Govan, a shipbuilding district of Glasgow, where his father worked in the shipyards. He was a fierce, hard-working striker for several Scottish clubs, and then a fierce, hard-working manager. At Aberdeen, a club nobody expected to win anything, he broke the grip of Scotland's two giants, Celtic and Rangers, winning three league titles. In 1983 Aberdeen even beat Real Madrid in a European final. Big English clubs started to notice.

On 6 November 1986, Manchester United appointed him manager. They were near the bottom of the league. They hadn't been champions since 1967, nineteen years earlier, and in that time Liverpool, their great rivals, had won the title again and again. Ferguson's first match was a 2–0 defeat at Oxford United. It was not a glorious beginning.

What he found shocked him. Some of the players drank far too much. Discipline was poor. The club's scouting network had shrunk, and its youth system wasn't producing enough good young players. Ferguson set about changing all of it at once. He brought in more scouts, demanded that they find the best boys in the north of England, and visited families personally to persuade them. On one boy's fourteenth birthday, Ferguson turned up at his house. The boy's name was Ryan Giggs.

But changing a club's culture takes years, and football is an impatient business. United finished 11th, then 2nd, then 11th again. In September 1989, City thrashed them 5–1 at Maine Road. Ferguson later wrote that he went home afterwards and hid his head under a pillow. It was one of the lowest moments of his whole career.

Seven days in January

By the new year, Ferguson's position was precarious. Every newspaper had a theory about who would replace him. Every defeat brought more scrutiny. And on 7 January 1990, United had to travel to Nottingham Forest in the third round of the FA Cup. Forest were one of the best cup teams in the country, managed by the legendary Brian Clough. They were at home. Most people expected them to win.

It was a scrappy, nervy match. Then, early in the second half, Mark Hughes sent over a cross from the left, and a young striker called Mark Robins stretched to meet it with his head. One–nil. United held on.

Robins wasn't even a regular in the team. He was 20, and he never became a star. But his header has gone down in United history, because it kept the club in the FA Cup, and the FA Cup was Ferguson's last chance to win something that season.

Myth or fact?

"Mark Robins's goal saved Alex Ferguson from the sack."

Verdict: nobody knows for sure. It's one of the most famous stories in football, and many newspapers at the time reported that Ferguson would be sacked if United went out. But Martin Edwards, the club's chairman, always insisted that the board had never planned to get rid of him. Ferguson himself has said that we will never know how close he came. What we do know is that the goal gave him breathing space, and a cup run gave him time. Sometimes in football, time is everything.

The road to Wembley

After Forest, United's path through the Cup was hard. They were drawn away from home in every round before the semi-final: Hereford on a mud-heap of a pitch, Newcastle in a thrilling 3–2, Sheffield United in the quarter-final. In the semi-final against Oldham, they drew 3–3 and then won the replay 2–1 in extra time. The winner? Mark Robins, of course.

The final, against Crystal Palace, was wild. United led, fell behind, equalised, and drew 3–3 after extra time. In those days a drawn final meant a replay, five days later.

Before the replay, Ferguson made one of the hardest decisions of his career. His goalkeeper, Jim Leighton, had played for him for years at Aberdeen and was a friend. But Leighton had made mistakes in the first game, and Ferguson dropped him, bringing in Les Sealey, a keeper on loan from Luton. Leighton was heartbroken. Some people thought it was cruel. Ferguson thought it was simply necessary: the team came first, always, even before friendship. It was the first clear sign of the ruthless streak that would make him the most successful manager in British history.

Sealey kept a clean sheet. In the 59th minute, left-back Lee Martin, who hardly ever scored, ran onto a long pass and smashed the ball into the roof of the net. United won 1–0. The FA Cup was theirs.

Five months after the bedsheet, Ferguson was vindicated. He had his first trophy as Manchester United manager.

Over the next twenty-three years, he would win thirty-seven more.
