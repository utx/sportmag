Chapter 3
Twenty-Six Years
Part Two: The Fergie Years
Rotterdam15 May
1991

Manchester United against Barcelona, in a European final.

It sounds like a dream fixture now. In 1991 it sounded almost impossible. English clubs had only just been allowed back into European competitions after a five-year ban, following a disaster at the 1985 European Cup final. Barcelona, managed by the Dutch genius Johan Cruyff, were on their way to becoming one of the best teams in the world. United were a team still finding out how good they could be.

The final of the Cup Winners' Cup was played in Rotterdam, in the Netherlands, and thousands of United fans made the trip. The man they were watching most closely was Mark Hughes. A few years earlier, Hughes had left United to play for Barcelona, and it had gone badly. He hadn't settled, hadn't scored enough, and had been sent away on loan. Now he was back in red, facing the club that had given up on him.

Midway through the second half, Steve Bruce headed towards goal. The ball was probably going in anyway, but Hughes made sure, poking it over the line. Then, a few minutes later, Hughes chased a through-ball, ran around the Barcelona goalkeeper, and from an angle so tight it seemed impossible, he smashed it into the net. Ronald Koeman pulled one back with a free kick, but United held on. They had won 2–1. Hughes had his revenge, and United had their first European trophy since 1968.

The one that got away

The next season, 1991–92, United finally had a real chance of the league title. Their drought had lasted 25 years. They won the League Cup, beating Nottingham Forest 1–0 in the final. They led the league for much of the season. And then, in the final weeks, with too many games crammed into too few days, they ran out of legs. They lost to Forest, then to West Ham, then to Liverpool. Leeds United, their bitter rivals from across the Pennine hills, won the title instead.

Losing to Leeds hurt. Losing the title at Anfield, with Liverpool fans celebrating United's misery, hurt even more. Ferguson said afterwards that the players had been exhausted, not frightened, and that they would learn from it. Plenty of people didn't believe him.

The long wait: 1967 to 1993

The phone call

In November 1992, as the story goes, a phone call changed everything. The managing director of Leeds rang Manchester United's chairman, Martin Edwards, to ask whether United would sell their defender Denis Irwin. Edwards said no. But Ferguson happened to be in the room, and he had an idea. He scribbled a note, and Edwards asked the question on it: would Leeds sell Eric Cantona?

Amazingly, they would. Cantona was a French striker with a brilliant touch, a famously fiery temper, and a long history of falling out with managers. He had just helped Leeds win the title, but they thought he was too much trouble. United paid £1.2 million for him. It turned out to be one of the greatest bargains in football history.

Cantona was enigmatic. He played with his collar turned up, he spoke in strange, poetic sentences, and he did things on the ball that English defenders had never seen before. But the most important thing he did was make everyone around him braver. Young players watched him train extra hours after everyone else had gone home, and started staying behind too. He was the catalyst Ferguson's team had been missing: the final piece of the puzzle.

Bruce, and the longest stoppage time

The 1992–93 season was the very first season of the brand-new Premier League, and this time United didn't fade. On 10 April 1993, they played Sheffield Wednesday at Old Trafford, and with four minutes left, they were losing 1–0. A defeat would have put their title chances in serious danger.

Then Steve Bruce, a centre-back who was famous for his broken nose and his bravery, rose to head in an equaliser. A draw would keep them alive. But the referee had been injured earlier and replaced, and a lot of time had been added on. The game kept going. Six minutes into stoppage time, Bruce rose again, and headed the winner.

Old Trafford, 10 April 1993: a Steve Bruce header beats the Sheffield Wednesday goalkeeper. Photo: Rex Features, used with permission.

Old Trafford exploded. Ferguson leapt off the bench. His assistant, Brian Kidd, ran onto the pitch and sank to his knees on the grass, arms in the air. Rival fans grumbled about how much time had been added. From then on, whenever United scored late, they had a name for it: "Fergie time".

Moments after the winner: Brian Kidd on his knees on the grass, and Alex Ferguson with both arms in the air. Photo used with permission.

Three weeks later, on 2 May, Ferguson was playing golf when he got the news. Aston Villa, the only team who could catch United, had lost at home to Oldham. Manchester United were champions of England for the first time in twenty-six years. The next night, Bruce and Bryan Robson lifted the first Premier League trophy at Old Trafford, and the euphoria could be heard all over Manchester.

The drought was over. United's talisman had a title medal with two different clubs in two years. And Ferguson was only getting started.
