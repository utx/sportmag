Noisy Neighbours
FA Cup final25 May

The FA Cup final. Manchester City against Manchester United. And City are supposed to win easily.

They're the champions of England, again. They've won the league four times in a row. They're chasing a second League and FA Cup Double in two years, and the bookmakers think United have about as much chance as a paper umbrella in a Manchester rainstorm.

Half an hour in, City's defenders get in a muddle. Joško Gvardiol tries to head the ball back to his goalkeeper, it falls short, and Alejandro Garnacho, United's 19-year-old Argentine winger, nips in, rounds the keeper and rolls it into the empty net. One–nil.

Nine minutes later, United break forward at speed, and Kobbie Mainoo, a 19-year-old from Stockport who came through United's academy, finishes a slick move by sliding the ball into the corner. Two–nil.

Garnacho and Mainoo are the first pair of teenagers ever to score in an FA Cup final. City get one back near the end, but it doesn't matter. United win 2–1, and the red half of Manchester goes wild.

There are few things in football quite as satisfying as beating City. This chapter explains why.

A century in the shadows

Manchester City began in 1880 as a church team called St Mark's, in West Gorton, and took the name Manchester City in 1894. For most of the next hundred years, they were the city's second club. They had some good teams and some good days, but United had the history, the glamour and the trophies.

City were so famous for messing things up that their own fans invented a phrase for it: "Typical City". In 1998 they were relegated to the third tier of English football, the lowest point in their history. While United were winning the Treble in 1999, City were playing in the same division as Macclesfield and Wycombe. For years, United fans hung a banner at Old Trafford that simply counted the number of seasons since City had last won a trophy. Every year the number went up by one.

Then the money arrived

On 1 September 2008, everything changed. Manchester City were bought by Sheikh Mansour, a member of the ruling family of Abu Dhabi, a small, oil-rich country in the Middle East. Overnight, City became one of the richest clubs in the world. They began buying famous players for enormous fees and paying them lavish wages.

The next summer, City signed Carlos Tevez from United and put up a giant sky-blue poster in Manchester city centre with his face on it and the words "Welcome to Manchester", as if United didn't exist. In September 2009, Michael Owen scored a winner for United in the 96th minute of a 4–3 derby, and Ferguson delivered his famous verdict on his neighbours:

"Sometimes you have a noisy neighbour. You cannot do anything about that. They will always be noisy."

But the noise kept getting louder. City won the FA Cup in 2011. In October that year they beat United 6–1 at Old Trafford. And on the last day of the 2011–12 season, United won at Sunderland and stood on the pitch, champions for a few seconds, waiting for the news from City's game against QPR. In the 94th minute, Sergio Agüero scored. The two clubs finished level on 89 points. City won the league on goal difference.

13 May 2012: City fans flood the pitch after Agüero's late goal. That title was won in one of the nine seasons City have now been found guilty of breaking the rules. Photo: Oldelpaso, CC BY-SA 3.0.

Counting that one, City have won the league eight times since 2012, and in 2023 they won a Treble of their own, twenty-four years after United showed them how.

115 charges, 114 proven

Football has rules about money. Clubs must tell the truth about how much they earn, from sponsors for example, and how much they pay their players and managers. There are limits on how much money a club can lose. The point is simple: to stop one club using unlimited money to buy success while everyone else plays fair.

In February 2023, after an investigation lasting around four years, the Premier League charged City with 115 breaches of its rules. Most of them covered nine seasons, from 2009–10 to 2017–18: the exact years in which City built their empire. They were accused of hiding where their money really came from, of not revealing what they paid players and managers, of breaking spending rules, and of refusing to cooperate with the investigation.

In September 2026, an independent commission of legal experts gave its verdict. City were found guilty of 114 of the 115 charges.

What this book thinks

This is a Manchester United book, written by United fans, and we promised not to be shy. So here it is, plainly:

All of Manchester City's success came from cheating.

(Yes, they won a few trophies long before the money arrived: two league titles in 128 years. We're not counting that as "success".)

Here's our case. Before September 2008, City had won the league twice in their entire history. After the money arrived, they were found guilty of breaking the financial rules again and again, for nine seasons in a row. Those were the seasons in which they bought their best players, built their training ground, hired some of the best-paid coaches in the world, and turned themselves into a winning machine. Every trophy they have won since was won by that machine. Take away the cheating, and the machine wouldn't exist.

That title in 2012, the one they took from United on goal difference? It was won in the middle of the guilty years. So was 2014. So was 2018. Every one of them is tainted.

And here's the part that makes it almost funny. Remember Chapter 6? More than a hundred years ago, City were caught secretly paying their players more than the rules allowed, and United ended up with their best players. Some habits, it seems, are hard to break.

The other side (because fair is fair)

City say they did nothing wrong. They have appealed against the verdict, saying the commission made mistakes, and the appeal is due to be heard by early 2027. Their punishment hasn't been decided yet either. For comparison, Everton and Nottingham Forest were docked points in 2023–24 for much smaller breaches, and some people think City could be fined hundreds of millions of pounds, have points taken away, or even lose titles.

City are entitled to their appeal. We're entitled not to believe them. An independent panel of experts studied the evidence for years and decided, charge after charge, that City broke the rules. That isn't jealousy. That's a verdict.

United's derby greatest hits

Whatever happens in the courtroom, United fans have plenty of happy derby memories to keep them warm:

  • 1990s and 2000s: a whole era of United dominance. Between 1993 and 2011 United won twelve league titles. City won none.
  • November 1994: United 5–0 City at Old Trafford. Cantona scored the first, Andrei Kanchelskis got a hat-trick and Mark Hughes added the fifth. It's still United's biggest win over City in a competitive match.
  • September 2009: Owen in the 96th minute. United 4–3 City. "Fergie time" at its finest.
  • February 2011: Wayne Rooney's overhead kick, a ball from Nani met in mid-air with an acrobatic scissor-kick into the top corner. It was later voted the best goal of the Premier League's first twenty seasons.
  • May 2024: Garnacho and Mainoo. The teenagers who stopped City's Double.

Word Roots: fin means "end"

This whole chapter is about finance, which is all about money. Strangely, the word comes from the Latin finis, meaning "end": finance was originally about ending a debt by paying it off. The same root gives us final (the last match), finish, finite (with an end) and infinite (without one). And a fine, which City may soon be paying, was once money paid to settle the end of a legal case.
