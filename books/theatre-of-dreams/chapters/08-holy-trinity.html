Chapter 8 · Flashback
The Holy Trinity
Wembley29 May
1968

Wembley. The European Cup final. One–one, and the clock is almost at ninety minutes.

Benfica of Portugal have just equalised, and now their greatest player, Eusébio, one of the most powerful strikers who ever lived, is running clean through on United's goal. Only the goalkeeper, Alex Stepney, stands between him and the trophy. Eusébio winds up and hits it as hard as he can. If it goes in, Benfica will almost certainly be champions of Europe.

Stepney catches it.

Not a dive, not a desperate fingertip save. He just holds on, clutching the ball to his chest. Eusébio is so impressed that he stops and applauds him.

Extra time. And in extra time, Manchester United are unstoppable.

Two minutes in, George Best picks up the ball, slips past a defender, dummies the goalkeeper so that he dives the wrong way, and strolls the ball into the empty net. Then Brian Kidd, celebrating his nineteenth birthday, heads in a third. Then Bobby Charlton, who had scored United's first goal with a rare header, flicks in a fourth. United win 4–1.

At the final whistle, Charlton and Matt Busby find each other on the pitch and hold on tight. Ten years earlier, both of them had nearly died at Munich. Now they are the first English club to win the European Cup. Busby, it seemed, had finally fulfilled the promise he made to the Babes.

Best, Law and Charlton

United fans call them the Holy Trinity, or the United Trinity. They were three very different players from three different countries, and each one was voted the best player in Europe.

Denis Law came from Aberdeen in Scotland. He was skinny, cheeky, fearless in the air and lightning-quick in the penalty area. He would hang onto his shirt cuffs with his fingers and then shoot one arm into the sky whenever he scored, which was often. The fans named him "the King of the Stretford End". Cruelly, a knee injury meant he missed the 1968 final; he watched it from a hospital bed.

Bobby Charlton came from a mining town in the north-east of England. He was a quiet, gentle man with a shot like a cannonball, and he survived Munich. In 1966 he helped England win the World Cup. For decades he held United's records for most appearances and most goals, and when he died in 2023, the whole of football mourned him. He is said to be the man who first called Old Trafford "the Theatre of Dreams".

George Best came from Belfast in Northern Ireland. United's scout there sent Busby a telegram that said, roughly: "I think I've found you a genius." He was right. Best was a skinny, long-haired winger who could dribble past entire teams, and he became football's first real celebrity. After he tore Benfica apart in Lisbon in 1966, a Portuguese newspaper called him "El Beatle", after the most famous band in the world.

The long wait

Winning the European Cup felt like the end of a long journey, and in a way it was. Busby retired the following year. Without him, United began a slow decline. Charlton and Law were getting older. Best, who found fame and pressure hard to cope with, began missing training and drinking too much. He played his last game for United in 1974, when he was only 27.

That same year, United were relegated to the Second Division. Six years after being the best team in Europe, they were out of the top flight.

Myth or fact?

"Denis Law relegated United with a backheel for City."

Verdict: myth. By 1974 Law had left United and joined City. In April that year, in the derby at Old Trafford, he scored for City with a cheeky backheel, and United lost 1–0. Law was so upset that he didn't celebrate; he walked off the pitch soon afterwards and it turned out to be his last goal in league football. But here's the thing: other results that day meant United were going down whatever happened. Law's goal didn't relegate them. It just felt that way.

But United's spell in purgatory lasted just one season. Under their bouncy, funny manager Tommy Docherty, they won the Second Division in 1975 with exciting, attacking football. And in 1977 they reached the FA Cup final against Liverpool, who had already won the league and were about to win the European Cup too. Liverpool wanted the Treble. United said no. They won the final 2–1, and Liverpool's Treble dream was over.

Under Ron Atkinson in the 1980s, United won the FA Cup twice more, in 1983 and 1985. The 1985 final was another spoiler: Everton, the champions, were chasing a Treble of their own, and Norman Whiteside's curling winner in extra time stopped them. In 1985–86, United won their first ten league games in a row, and everyone thought the long wait for a title was over.

It wasn't. They faded and finished fourth. Early the next season they were near the bottom of the table, and in November 1986 Atkinson was sacked.

The club needed someone new. Someone tough. Someone from Scotland, perhaps, who had built a winning team from nothing.

And that, as you already know, is where this book's story began.
