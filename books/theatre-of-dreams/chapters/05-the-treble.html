Chapter 5
Football, Bloody Hell
Camp Nou, Barcelona26 May
1999

Ninety minutes gone. Bayern Munich 1, Manchester United 0.

Ninety thousand people are inside the Camp Nou in Barcelona for the Champions League final, and most of the United fans among them have stopped believing. Their team has been losing since the sixth minute, when Mario Basler curled a free kick past Peter Schmeichel. Bayern have hit the post. Bayern have hit the crossbar. United's two best midfielders, Roy Keane and Paul Scholes, are sitting in the stand, banned. Somewhere in the stadium, officials are already tying ribbons in Bayern's colours onto the handles of the trophy.

Up in the stand, the president of UEFA leaves his seat and heads down towards the pitch, so that he'll be ready to hand the cup to Bayern's captain.

On the touchline, the fourth official lifts his electronic board. It shows how many minutes of stoppage time are left. The number is 3.

United win a corner. Schmeichel, their giant goalkeeper, playing his very last game for the club, sprints the length of the pitch and into Bayern's penalty area. David Beckham swings the ball in. It's cleared, but only as far as Ryan Giggs, who scuffs a shot with his weaker foot. It's going wide. It's going nowhere. And then Teddy Sheringham, a substitute, sticks out his right foot and turns it into the bottom corner.

One–one. Ninety-one minutes. The United end goes delirious. Extra time, surely.

But United don't want extra time. They go straight back up the pitch. Ole Gunnar Solskjær, another substitute, wins another corner. Beckham swings it in again. Sheringham flicks it on with his head, and Solskjær, at the far post, sticks out a toe.

Another corner, another scramble: the Bayern penalty area in those last frantic minutes. Photo: picture-alliance / empics, used with permission.

The ball flies into the roof of the net.

The winner: Solskjær's outstretched toe sends the ball into the roof of the net. Photo: Alain Gadoffre / Getty Images, used with permission.

Two–one. Ninety-three minutes. Bayern's players collapse onto the grass. One of them, Sammy Kuffour, beats the turf with his fists. When the UEFA president finally reaches the pitch, the scene in front of him has been turned upside down. He is reported to have said: "The winners are crying, and the losers are dancing."

The three minutes

A few minutes later, a television reporter pushed a microphone towards Alex Ferguson and asked him how he felt. Ferguson, who was never lost for words, was lost for words. Finally, he managed three:

"Football. Bloody hell."

("Bloody" is a mild swear word in Britain and Australia. You might want to check with a grown-up before using it at school.)

There was one more thing that made the night feel like destiny. Sir Matt Busby, the man who had built Manchester United, was born on 26 May 1909. If he had still been alive, the night United became champions of Europe would have been his ninetieth birthday.

How they got there

That night in Barcelona was the end of the most extraordinary eleven days in the club's history. United had won the Premier League on 16 May, coming from behind to beat Tottenham 2–1 on the final day and finish a single point ahead of Arsenal. Six days later, they beat Newcastle 2–0 in the FA Cup final. Barcelona made it three. No English club had ever won the league, the FA Cup and the European Cup in the same season. They called it the Treble.

Champions of Europe: United's players, medals round their necks, with the trophy on the Camp Nou pitch. Photo used with permission.

The goals came from one of the happiest partnerships United have ever had. Dwight Yorke, a grinning striker from Tobago, arrived in the summer of 1998 and clicked with Andy Cole almost at once. They played with a rapport that looked like a game between two best friends in a park: flicks, one-twos, back-heels, and always a big smile. Between them they scored 53 goals that season, Yorke 29 and Cole 24.

Dwight Yorke and Andy Cole with the Premier League trophy. Between them they scored 53 goals in the Treble season. Photo used with permission.

But two nights in April had made it possible.

The wonder goal (14 April, Villa Park)

The FA Cup semi-final replay against Arsenal, the reigning champions. It was a brutal, bad-tempered game. Roy Keane was sent off. In the very last minute of normal time, Arsenal won a penalty, and if Dennis Bergkamp scored, United were out. Schmeichel dived and saved it.

Then, in extra time, Giggs picked up a loose pass in his own half and set off. He went past one Arsenal defender. Then another. Then another.

The run: Giggs (in white) carries the ball straight at the Arsenal defence. Photo used with permission.

Arsenal's back line was one of the best in history, and Giggs ran straight through all of it before smashing the ball past David Seaman, England's goalkeeper, and into the roof of the net.

The finish: Giggs smashes the ball past Seaman as an Arsenal defender slides in, too late. Photo used with permission.

He ripped off his shirt and ran down the touchline, whirling it around his head, chest hair and all, with his team-mates chasing after him.

The celebration: Giggs whirls his shirt as Dwight Yorke (number 19) races after him. Photo used with permission.

It was one of the greatest goals ever scored in English football, and it came in the last FA Cup semi-final replay ever played.

Many fans still call it the greatest FA Cup goal of all time. Photo used with permission.

The captain's night (21 April, Turin)

The Champions League semi-final, second leg, away to Juventus, one of the best teams in Europe. After eleven minutes, United were 2–0 down. Their dream of a Treble looked finished.

Roy Keane, United's captain, refused to accept it. In the 24th minute he leapt above everyone to meet a Beckham corner and glance a header inside the far post. Two–one.

The fightback starts: Keane's header beats the Juventus goalkeeper. Photo: Allsport, used with permission.

Keane wheels away after his header. Game on. Photo used with permission.

Ten minutes later came the cruellest moment of his career. A loose pass gave the ball to Zinedine Zidane, Juventus's French superstar, and Keane, stretching to win it back, clipped him. The referee showed him a yellow card. It was his second of the competition, which meant that whatever happened now, he would miss the final.

The yellow card that cost Keane the final. Zidane, the player he fouled, is getting up on the right. Photo used with permission.

Most players would have sulked. Keane did the opposite. He ran the rest of the game, tackling, organising, driving his team forward, as if he was dragging United to a final he knew he could never play in. Moments after his booking, Dwight Yorke headed the equaliser. Near the end, Andy Cole scored the winner. United won 3–2.

Alex Ferguson never forgot it. Years later, he wrote that it was:

"The most emphatic display of selflessness I have seen on a football field."

It's still talked about as one of the most selfless performances by any United captain, and one of the most indomitable.

Word Roots: tri- means "three"

The Treble comes from an old word meaning "triple". The same root shows up all over this book and all over English:

  • triple: three times as much;
  • trio: a group of three, like the Holy Trinity of Best, Law and Charlton in Chapter 8;
  • triangle: a shape with three angles;
  • triathlon: a race in three parts (swim, cycle, run);
  • tripod: a stand with three legs (pod means "foot").

Can you think of any more?

Ferguson was knighted a few weeks later. From then on he was Sir Alex.

Twenty-four years later, Manchester City won a Treble of their own. Chapter 11 has a few things to say about how they paid for it. But United will always have one thing City can never take away from them: they were first.
