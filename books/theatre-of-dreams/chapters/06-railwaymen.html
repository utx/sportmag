Railwaymen to Red Devils
Part Three: Where it all began
Newton Heath1878

Spring 1902. The club is dying.

It owes more than £2,500, an enormous sum at the time. It can't pay its players. A court has ordered it to be shut down. Its captain, Harry Stafford, is desperately trying to raise money, and he has organised a fundraising fair, called a bazaar, to help.

Stafford has a big St Bernard dog called Major, who wanders around the bazaar with a collecting tin tied to his collar. One night, so the story goes, Major wanders off. He's found by a wealthy brewery owner called John Henry Davies, whose daughter falls in love with him. Stafford goes to fetch his dog, gets talking to Davies, and persuades him to save the football club.

It's a wonderful story. Parts of it might even be true. What we know for certain is that Davies and four other businessmen each put £500 into the club, and that they gave it a new start, new colours and a new name: Manchester United.

Without a lost dog, and a captain who refused to give up, there might be no Manchester United at all.

Green, gold and smoke

The club began in 1878, when workers from the Lancashire and Yorkshire Railway's carriage and wagon works at Newton Heath, in north-east Manchester, formed a football team. They were called Newton Heath LYR, and they played in shirts that were half green and half gold. (United fans still wear green and gold scarves sometimes, as a reminder of where the club came from.)

The Stretford End today. The flag in the middle says "1878 M16": the year the club began, and Old Trafford's postcode. Photo used with permission.

For their first home, Newton Heath played on a bumpy pitch at North Road. In 1893 they moved to Bank Street in Clayton, right next to a chemical works whose chimneys pumped clouds of smoke over the pitch. Visiting teams complained that they couldn't breathe. Newton Heath joined the Football League in 1892, but they were never very good, and they were always short of money. By 1902 they were insolvent, which is where our story began.

The first golden age

With Davies as their benefactor, United became ambitious. They hired a clever manager, Ernest Mangnall. And in 1906 they got lucky, thanks to their neighbours.

Manchester City, it turned out, had been breaking the rules about money. Back then, there was a maximum wage in English football, and City were found guilty of secretly paying their players more than they were allowed. Their star winger, Billy Meredith, was also banned for trying to bribe an opponent. The football authorities punished City heavily, and their best players were banned from ever playing for the club again. When their bans ended, four of them, including Meredith, crossed the city and joined United.

(City breaking the money rules? Remember this. It will come up again.)

With Meredith on the wing, United won their first league title in 1908, the FA Cup in 1909 and a second title in 1911. Davies spent a fortune on a magnificent new stadium, designed by the famous architect Archibald Leitch, on a site next to a railway line and a canal in a district called Old Trafford. It opened on 19 February 1910, with room for around 80,000 people.

The visitors that day were Liverpool. They won 4–3. Some guests have no manners.

Bombs and borrowed homes

The golden age didn't last. Through the 1920s and 1930s, United bounced between the First and Second Divisions, and in 1931 they nearly went bust again until another businessman, James Gibson, came to the rescue. In 1934 they only avoided dropping into the Third Division on the last day of the season.

Then came the Second World War. Old Trafford was close to Trafford Park, a huge industrial area making supplies for the war, and German bombers came at night to attack it. On 11 March 1941, bombs fell on Old Trafford itself. The main stand was destroyed, and the pitch was scorched.

For years afterwards, United had to play their home games at Maine Road, the home of Manchester City, and pay them rent. Imagine that: United fans had to go to City's ground to watch their own team. They didn't move back to Old Trafford until 1949.

Enter Mr Busby

In 1945, United appointed a new manager. His name was Matt Busby, and he was a Scottish former miner's son who had been a fine player for, of all clubs, Manchester City and Liverpool. That should have made United fans nervous. Instead, it turned out to be the best decision the club ever made.

Sir Matt Busby's statue outside Old Trafford. He was United's manager for 24 years, longer than anyone except Sir Alex Ferguson. Photo: Bernt Rostad, CC BY 2.0.

Busby had the audacity to ask for something no manager in England had: complete control over picking the team, running the training and buying the players. Until then, club directors had often picked the team themselves. Busby said no. The football would be his job. The directors agreed, and it helped create the modern idea of what a football manager does.

In 1948, Busby's first great team won the FA Cup, beating Blackpool 4–2 in a final many people still call one of the best ever. In 1952, United were league champions for the first time in 41 years. And Busby was already building something even better: a team of teenagers who would become the most famous young side in the world.

Myth or fact?

"United have always been called the Red Devils."

Verdict: myth. In the early days United were sometimes called "the Heathens", after Newton Heath. "Red Devils" came later, in the 1960s, when Matt Busby borrowed it from Salford's rugby league team, who had been nicknamed "les Diables Rouges" (French for "the Red Devils") on a tour of France in 1934. Busby reportedly thought a devil sounded more frightening for opponents than a "Babe". In the early 1970s a little red devil, holding a pitchfork, was added to the club badge, and apart from a short break around the club's hundredth birthday in 1978, it has been there ever since.

The badge today: the ship comes from the coat of arms of the city of Manchester, and the devil comes from Salford. Badge used with permission.
