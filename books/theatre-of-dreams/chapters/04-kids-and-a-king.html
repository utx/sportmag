Chapter 4
Kids and a King
Part Two: The Fergie Years
Match of the Day19 Aug
1995

Aston Villa 3, Manchester United 1.

It was the first day of the season, and United had been taken apart. That night, on Match of the Day, the BBC's football programme, the presenter asked the former Liverpool captain Alan Hansen what he made of it. Hansen didn't hold back. Over the summer, Ferguson had sold three of his star players, Mark Hughes, Paul Ince and Andrei Kanchelskis, and replaced them with nobody. Instead he was picking teenagers.

Hansen shook his head, and said the sentence that has followed him around ever since:

"You can't win anything with kids."

He was wrong. He was spectacularly, gloriously, hilariously wrong. And the kids he was talking about went on to win more than almost any group of players in English history.

The Class of '92

The "kids" had a name. In 1992, United's youth team won the FA Youth Cup, and the boys from that team and the squad around it became known as the Class of '92. There were six of them, and all of them would play for England or Wales: Ryan Giggs, David Beckham, Paul Scholes, Nicky Butt, and two brothers, Gary and Phil Neville.

Most of them had grown up together, travelling to training on the same buses, playing in the same youth teams and learning from the same coaches. Ferguson's youth coach, Eric Harrison, was famously strict, and demanded perfection even when his team was winning easily. By the time they reached the first team, they knew each other's games by heart. That was the secret Hansen hadn't understood. These weren't just kids. They were a team that had been built over years.

The King

The kids had a leader, too: Eric Cantona, whom United's fans called simply "The King". In 1993–94 he led United to the Double, the league title and the FA Cup in the same season, scoring two penalties in a 4–0 final against Chelsea.

But Cantona had a dark side, and on 25 January 1995 the whole world saw it. At Crystal Palace he was sent off for kicking out at a defender. As he walked towards the tunnel, a Palace supporter ran down to the front of the stand and screamed abuse at him. Cantona snapped. He launched himself over the advertising hoardings, feet first, in a flying "kung-fu" kick, and then started throwing punches.

It was one of the most notorious moments in the history of English football. Whatever the fan had shouted, and however much he had tried to provoke Cantona, it was wrong, and Cantona was punished heavily. He was banned from football for eight months and ordered to do 120 hours of community service, which he spent coaching children. Without him, United lost the league title by a single point on the last day of the season, and the FA Cup final too.

Then came the strangest press conference ever held. Cantona walked in, sat down, sipped some water, and said, slowly:

"When the seagulls follow the trawler, it is because they think sardines will be thrown into the sea."

Then he got up and walked out.

Reporters spent days arguing about what he meant. Most people think it was a metaphor: the journalists were the seagulls, following him around like birds chasing a fishing boat (the trawler), hoping for juicy stories (the sardines). Whatever it meant, it was pure Cantona.

Love it!

Cantona came back on 1 October 1995, against Liverpool of all teams. Within two minutes he had set up a goal, and later he scored a penalty. The King had returned, and the kids were ready for him.

That season turned into one of the great title races. By January, Kevin Keegan's Newcastle, who played thrilling, attacking football, were twelve points clear. Everyone thought it was over. But United kept chasing, and in March they went to Newcastle and won 1–0. The goal? Cantona, of course. Newcastle began to wobble. In late April, after a game against Leeds, Keegan lost his temper live on television, jabbing his finger at the camera and shouting about Ferguson: "I will love it if we beat them! Love it!"

They didn't beat them. On the last day of the season, United won 3–0 at Middlesbrough and were champions again.

A week later came the FA Cup final, against Liverpool. Liverpool's players turned up at Wembley wearing cream-coloured designer suits, which became one of the most laughed-about outfits in football history. Their football was about as impressive as their fashion. With five minutes left, a corner was punched out to the edge of the box, and Cantona, now United's captain, adjusted his feet and volleyed it through a crowd of players into the net. One–nil. United had won the Double for the second time in three years, the first English club ever to do it twice.

The irony was delicious. The man who said "you can't win anything with kids" was a former Liverpool captain. The kids had just beaten Liverpool in the Cup final to complete the Double.

The next season the kids won the league again, and Beckham scored from the halfway line against Wimbledon. Then, in May 1997, at the age of just 30, Cantona announced that he was retiring from football. He had played five seasons for United and won four league titles. The King had left the building. But the kids were growing up, and they were about to do something nobody had ever done before.
