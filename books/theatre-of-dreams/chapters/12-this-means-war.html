Four–three. One hundred and twenty-one minutes. Amad is so happy that he rips off his shirt, gets a second yellow card, and is sent off. He doesn't care, and neither does anyone else in red. Liverpool's dream of four trophies is over, and United are in the semi-finals.

That's what this rivalry does to people.

Older than football

Manchester and Liverpool are two big north-western cities about 35 miles apart, and they were rivals long before either football club existed. In the 1800s, Liverpool had the port, and Manchester's factories had to pay Liverpool's fees to get their goods in and out by sea. Manchester's businessmen got so fed up that they built the Manchester Ship Canal, a waterway more than 35 miles long, so that ships could sail straight into Manchester and skip Liverpool entirely. It opened in 1894. Liverpool's merchants were furious.

When the football clubs became the two most successful in England, the rivalry turned into something close to war.

Their era, and ours

We'll be honest: Liverpool had a great team in the 1970s and 1980s. Between 1973 and 1990 they won the league eleven times and the European Cup four times. Liverpool fans will tell you about it. Often. In 1990 they had eighteen league titles, and United had seven.

Then Ferguson arrived, and something wonderful happened. Liverpool didn't win the league again for thirty years. In that time, United won it thirteen times. When Liverpool finally won another title in 2020, it was during the pandemic, and they had to celebrate it in an empty stadium.

Every Premier League champion from 1992–93 to 2024–25. (Arsenal won it in 2025–26.) Count the United badges: thirteen. Count Liverpool's: two. Graphic used with permission.

Liverpool won the league again in 2025. That made twenty titles, the same as United. Their fans were very excited about it. It's worth pointing out, politely, that it took them thirty-five years to get from eighteen to twenty, and that United got there first, in 2013.

Where the line is

The animosity between the two clubs is real, and most of it is good fun: songs, banter, arguments about who's best, and the exhilarating feeling of beating them. But you already know, from Chapter 7, where the line is. Both clubs have lost people in terrible disasters: United at Munich in 1958, Liverpool at Hillsborough in 1989. Real fans, on both sides, never joke about those.

The other lot: Leeds United

United's third great rivals live across the Pennine hills in Yorkshire. Lancashire and Yorkshire have been rivals for more than five hundred years: in the 1400s, two royal families, the House of Lancaster and the House of York, fought each other for the English throne in the Wars of the Roses, the red rose of Lancaster against the white rose of York.

In the 1960s and 70s, Leeds had a brilliant team that also had a reputation for kicking people, which earned them the nickname "Dirty Leeds". In 1992, Leeds pipped United to the title, and then, a few months later, sold United Eric Cantona for £1.2 million. Leeds fans have been trying to forget about that ever since. Later, two Leeds stars, Rio Ferdinand and Alan Smith, moved to Old Trafford too. Leeds fans took that rather badly as well.

In the 2000s Leeds ran out of money and dropped all the way to the third tier. They're back in the Premier League now, and the grudge is as fierce as ever.

The all-time XI argument

Here's a game every United fan plays: pick the club's greatest-ever team. Only eleven places, and more legends than you could fit into three teams. The football site Score 90 picked this one:

One all-time XI: Schmeichel; Neville, Stam, Ferdinand, Irwin; Charlton, Scholes, Giggs; Best, Rooney, Ronaldo. Graphic: Score 90, used with permission.

It's a frightening team. But it's also contentious, because look who's missing. No Eric Cantona, the King who changed everything in 1992. No Roy Keane, the captain who dragged United into the 1999 final. No Denis Law, who stands beside Best and Charlton on the statue outside Old Trafford. No Duncan Edwards, the player Bobby Charlton said was the only one who ever made him feel inferior. No Nemanja Vidić, no Bryan Robson, no Edwin van der Sar.

Roy Keane, captain's armband on, roaring as he piles onto a goal celebration with Ryan Giggs (11) and team-mates. Would he get into your all-time XI? Photo used with permission.

That's the fun of it. Every choice means leaving out somebody brilliant, and there's no right answer, only good arguments. So grab a pencil and pick your own. Who would you drop to make room for Cantona?

The next chapter

This book began with two teenagers who arrived in Manchester and became legends. It travelled back through a manager who nearly lost his job and then won everything; a Frenchman with his collar up; three minutes in Barcelona; a lost dog, a bombed stadium and a team of Babes; the bravest goalkeeper in the world; and the three statues that stand outside Old Trafford today.

Somewhere right now, there's a teenager in United's academy who will be in the next edition of this book. Maybe they'll score in a final. Maybe they'll beat City on the last day of the season, or knock Liverpool out of the Cup in the 121st minute. Maybe they'll be the first to play in the new stadium.

The story of Manchester United isn't finished. It never is. That's what makes it a theatre of dreams.
