Chapter 9
Moscow and Number Twenty
Part Four: Glory, Goodbye and Now
Moscow21 May
2008

It's well past midnight in Moscow, and it's pouring with rain.

Manchester United and Chelsea have played 120 minutes of the Champions League final, the first ever between two English clubs, and it's 1–1. Ronaldo headed United in front; Frank Lampard equalised just before half-time. Chelsea's star striker, Didier Drogba, has been sent off for slapping Nemanja Vidić. And now it's penalties.

Carlos Tevez scores. Michael Carrick scores. Then Cristiano Ronaldo, the best player in the world, steps up, does his famous stuttering run-up, and Petr Čech saves it. United's fans put their heads in their hands. Every Chelsea player scores, one after another, and then it all comes down to one kick.

If Chelsea's captain, John Terry, scores, Chelsea are champions of Europe.

Terry runs up. As he plants his standing foot on the sodden turf, it slides out from under him. He topples backwards, the ball skews off the outside of the post, and Terry falls to the ground. On the halfway line, United's players, arms linked, can hardly believe what they've just seen.

Sudden death. Anderson scores for United. Salomon Kalou scores for Chelsea. Then Ryan Giggs, playing his 759th game for Manchester United, more than anyone in the club's history, places his penalty calmly into the corner.

Nicolas Anelka walks up for Chelsea. Edwin van der Sar, United's tall, calm Dutch goalkeeper, guesses right, dives, and saves it.

The hero of the shoot-out: Edwin van der Sar (in green) leads the celebrations in Moscow. Photo used with permission.

Manchester United are champions of Europe for the third time. And it is fifty years, almost to the season, since the Munich air disaster.

The end of an era

That season, United had won the league as well, making it a European Double. The next year they won the league again, a third title in a row, and became world club champions in Japan. But in 2009 they lost the Champions League final to Barcelona, and Ronaldo left for Real Madrid. For the first time in years, the future looked uncertain.

Ferguson had seen this before. He rebuilt again, around Rooney, Giggs, Scholes, and two of the best centre-backs in the world: Rio Ferdinand, cool and elegant, and Vidić, a Serbian who threw himself in front of everything. In 2011, United won the league for the nineteenth time.

That number mattered enormously. Liverpool had eighteen league titles. For most of Ferguson's career, they had been the most successful club in England, and in 1986 they had been miles ahead. Nine years before, in 2002, Ferguson had been asked about his greatest challenge, and he had answered with one of the most famous quotes in football:

"My greatest challenge was knocking Liverpool right off their perch."

(The real quote has a very rude word just before "perch". We've left it out. You can probably guess.)

Now he had done it, emphatically. United had more league titles than anyone in England.

Number twenty

In 2012, United lost the title to Manchester City on goal difference, in the most painful way imaginable. You can read about that, and what we now know about City, in Chapter 11. Ferguson's response was typical. He went out and bought the best striker in the country, Arsenal's captain Robin van Persie, and in April 2013 van Persie scored a hat-trick against Aston Villa, including a stunning volley over his shoulder from a Rooney pass, to win United's twentieth league title.

The last one: Sir Alex Ferguson lifts the Premier League trophy at Old Trafford in May 2013, surrounded by his players. Photo used with permission.

Two weeks later, Ferguson announced that he was retiring.

Twenty league titles

Goodbye

On 12 May 2013, after his last home game, Ferguson took the microphone in the middle of the Old Trafford pitch. He thanked the players, the staff and the fans. He reminded everyone that when he arrived, the club had been through hard times, and that the fans had stuck with him. Then he gave them one last instruction:

"Your job now is to stand by our new manager."

His final game in charge, a week later, was a crazy 5–5 draw at West Brom. In twenty-six and a half years, he had won 38 trophies, including 13 league titles, 5 FA Cups and 2 Champions Leagues. Many people think he was the greatest manager in the history of football. This book certainly does.

Wayne Rooney, United's all-time record goalscorer, in 2008. Photo: Tsutomu Takasu, CC BY 2.0.

There was one more record still to fall. On 21 January 2017, deep into stoppage time at Stoke, Wayne Rooney curled a free kick into the top corner. It was his 250th goal for Manchester United, and it broke Sir Bobby Charlton's record, which had stood for more than forty years. Rooney left that summer with 253 goals, more than anyone in the club's history.
