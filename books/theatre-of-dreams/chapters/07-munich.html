The Babes and Munich
Remembered forever6 Feb
1958

This chapter tells the story of a real disaster in which people lost their lives. It doesn't describe anything gruesome, but it is sad. You might like to read it with a grown-up.

Belgrade, 5 February 1958. The best young team in Europe is celebrating.

Manchester United have just drawn 3–3 with Red Star Belgrade, in what was then Yugoslavia, and it's enough to put them into the semi-finals of the European Cup for the second year running. Their players are mostly in their early twenties. They are already the champions of England, twice over. Everyone in football thinks they are about to become the best team in the world.

They have a nickname: the Busby Babes.

The Babes

Matt Busby believed something that most managers at the time thought was mad: that if you found the best boys in the country at fifteen or sixteen, taught them well and trusted them, they would grow into a better team than any you could buy. His scouts searched the whole country. His assistant, a fiery Welshman called Jimmy Murphy, coached the youngsters for hours every day.

It worked brilliantly. United won the league in 1956 and again in 1957 with a team packed with home-grown young players. The best of all was Duncan Edwards, a midfielder from Dudley in the Midlands who made his United debut at 16 and played for England at 18. People who saw him play still describe him as the most complete footballer they ever watched: strong, fast, clever, and able to play anywhere. Bobby Charlton, who played alongside him, said Edwards was the only player who ever made him feel inferior.

Busby was visionary in another way, too. In 1956 the Football League didn't want English clubs to enter the new European Cup. Busby insisted on it. In their first-ever European home game, United beat Anderlecht of Belgium 10–0. Europe had been warned.

The crash

On the way home from Belgrade, the team's plane landed at Munich, in West Germany, to refuel. It was snowing. Twice the pilots tried to take off, and twice they stopped because of a problem with the engines. On the third attempt, the plane couldn't reach take-off speed in the slush that had built up on the runway. It ran off the end, through a fence, and crashed.

Twenty-three people died. Among them were eight Manchester United players.

Also remembered: club secretary Walter Crickmer, trainer Tom Curry and coach Bert Whalley; eight journalists, two crew members and two other passengers. Twenty-three people in all.

Duncan Edwards survived the crash itself, and for fifteen days doctors fought to save him. He died on 21 February 1958. He was 21 years old.

Matt Busby was so badly injured that a priest twice gave him the last rites, the prayers said for someone who is expected to die. He survived, but it was weeks before anyone dared tell him how many of his players were gone.

Harry Gregg

In the middle of the worst day in the club's history, there was heroism.

Harry Gregg was United's goalkeeper, a big, tough Northern Irishman who had only joined the club a few weeks earlier. When the plane came to a stop, he found himself alive, in a broken section of the aircraft. Instead of running to safety, as everyone was shouting at him to do, he went back in.

He heard a baby crying, and found her in the wreckage. He carried her out. Then he went back again for her mother, who was expecting another baby, and brought her out too. Then he went to help his team-mates, dragging Bobby Charlton and Dennis Viollet clear of the plane, and he stayed with Busby until help came. Gregg always said he wasn't a hero, and that he simply did what anyone would do. Almost nobody believed him.

Keep the flag flying

Jimmy Murphy should have been on that plane. He had stayed behind because he was also the manager of Wales, who had an important game in Cardiff the same day. Now, with Busby in hospital, it was Murphy who had to hold the club together.

He was grieving for the young men he had coached since they were boys, and yet he had a team to put out. Thirteen days after the crash, United played Sheffield Wednesday in the FA Cup. The match programme had a blank space where the United team should have been listed, because nobody knew who would play. Murphy put together a side of survivors, reserves, youth-team players and two new signings. Carried by a crowd full of grief and love, they won 3–0.

That makeshift team reached the FA Cup final that season. They lost to Bolton, but it hardly mattered. The club had survived. Its resilience became part of what Manchester United means.

And Busby came back. He walked on crutches at first, and he thought seriously about never returning to football. But he did, and he started building again, around a quiet survivor called Bobby Charlton. Ten years later, they would finish what the Babes had started. That story is in the next chapter.

The Munich clock at Old Trafford. Every year on 6 February, United's fans, players and staff gather to remember. Photo: David Dixon, CC BY-SA 2.0.

Today, the Munich clock on the corner of Old Trafford shows the date of the disaster, and a memorial plaque nearby lists the names of everyone who died. Every 6 February, at 3.04pm, the time of the crash, there is a solemn remembrance service. There is a memorial in Munich, too, on a street the German city named Manchesterplatz. And whenever the team plays near the anniversary, the whole stadium falls silent. It is one of the most poignant sounds in sport: seventy thousand people saying nothing at all.

The songs that should never be sung

There's one more part of this story, and it's an ugly one.

For decades, some fans of United's rivals have sung songs mocking the Munich disaster and celebrating the deaths of the players. Some make aeroplane gestures at United supporters. It has come from some Liverpool fans and some Manchester City fans, United's two biggest rivals, and from fans of Leeds and other clubs as well. It's a disgusting thing to do: laughing at young men who died, and at families who lost sons, brothers and fathers. Sadly, it has become a part of these rivalries, and every United fan has heard it. It is also against the rules. Clubs condemn it, and supporters have been arrested and banned from football grounds for doing it.

But this book promised to tell the truth, so here is the rest of it. Some United fans have sung songs that are just as cruel about Hillsborough, a disaster in 1989 in which 97 Liverpool supporters died at a football match. In 2022 United called those chants "completely unacceptable". The year after, the managers of United and Liverpool, Erik ten Hag and Jürgen Klopp, made a joint plea to both sets of fans to stop what is now called "tragedy chanting".

Be as loud as you like about your team. Be as rude as you like about the other lot's football. But never, ever make fun of people who died.
