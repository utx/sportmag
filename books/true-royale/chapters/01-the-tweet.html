True story #1
The tweet that hacked itself

At four o'clock on a Sunday morning in August, while almost everyone in Sydney was fast asleep, the Fortnite Island broke.

It happened during a live event called Unstable, the grand finale of the previous season. Millions of players around the world watched the scientist Dr Slone fire a gigantic beam at the damaged Zero Point, the glowing heart of the Island, to repair it. Then the villain Geno hacked in through a secret back door. Everybody's screen went haywire. The game's graphics fell apart, health bars drained by themselves, and silly pop-up adverts covered the screen ("Adopt a rubber duck", "Battle Bus rentals: driver NOT included") which players had to click away one by one. Then the picture cut to an old-fashioned computer start-up screen, and the Zero Point was gone. In its place sat a gigantic game cartridge.

That was strange enough. But about an hour later, something even stranger happened. The official Fortnite account on X, followed by millions of people, posted a message that looked as if it had been typed by somebody who didn't know what they were doing:

"seems useful… whatever it means lol"

Underneath was a block of what looked like computer gibberish.

The story, as fans soon worked out, was that Geno had "hijacked" Fortnite's social media accounts and posted a scrambled secret before Epic "regained control". It was all part of the season's storyline, of course: Epic's own way of building excitement before the new season began five days later. But the puzzle inside it was completely real. And you don't need a computer to solve it. You just need to know how computers think about letters.

How computers spell

Deep down, computers only understand numbers. So, decades ago, programmers agreed on a code called ASCII, in which every letter, digit and punctuation mark has its own number. Capital A is 65, capital B is 66, and so on, all the way up to Z, which is 90. A space is 32.

Programmers often write those numbers in hexadecimal, or "hex" for short. Our normal counting system uses ten digits, so when you reach nine, you run out and have to start a new column: 10. Hex uses sixteen digits, 0 to 9 followed by A, B, C, D, E and F, so it doesn't need a new column until it reaches sixteen. In hex, the number 65 is written 41, which means four sixteens and one more. So in the code, "41" means A, "42" means B, and "52" means R.

Armed with that, the first line of Geno's message stops being gibberish. The pairs 52 55 4C 45 53 45 54 spell R, U, L, E, S, E, T. Then 20 is a space. Then 55 4E 42 4F 55 4E 44 spells U, N, B, O, U, N, D. The first line says RULESET UNBOUND. The second line says RESTORING KEY //. (The numbers at the very start of each line, 7F00, 7F10 and 7F20, are just "addresses", like line numbers, telling you where in the computer's memory each row lives.)

The missing letters

The third line is where it gets properly tricky. It reads 4F 56 ?? 52 52 ?? 44 ?? 58 ??, which decodes to O V _ R R _ D _ X _. This is the "key" the second line promised, but four of its letters have been scrambled into question marks. The message even admits it: "RECOVERED 6 / 10" means six letters survived and four didn't. And "LEN 0A" tells you the key's length. In hex, A means ten, so 0A is simply 10. Ten letters.

So how could anyone possibly work out the missing four? That's what the last clue is for. CRC32 37CFAD9D is a checksum. A checksum is a kind of fingerprint for a piece of data: you feed the data into a particular calculation, and out comes a number. Change even one letter and the number comes out completely different. Computers use checksums constantly, to make sure files haven't been damaged while they were being downloaded.

Here, the checksum works like the answer at the back of a puzzle book. You guess the four missing letters, calculate the fingerprint of your guess, and if it comes out as 37CFAD9D, you've got it.

Now, you could just start guessing at random. But with 26 possible letters in each of four gaps, there are 456,976 possible combinations, and that would take a while even for a fast typist. A clever decipherer thinks instead. What ten-letter word would make sense in a season about hacking the rules? O V _ R R _ D _ … Say it out loud. The answer jumps out: OVERRIDE, with X and one more letter on the end. And what do players always want more of? XP. OVERRIDEXP.

We checked it ourselves with a computer. The checksum of OVERRIDEXP is exactly 37CFAD9D. Even better, we asked the computer to try all 456,976 possible combinations, and OVERRIDEXP was the only one that matched. Puzzle solved.

What the key unlocked

Five days later, on 20 August, the new season arrived, and with it came a brand-new button in the Battle Royale lobby: the Admin Panel. Type in a secret code, which Epic calls a Lobby Hack, and you get a reward. And one of the very first codes that worked was OverrideXP, worth 40,000 XP. The "hacked" tweet hadn't been nonsense at all. It had been a treasure map.

Lobby Hacks turned out to be one of the most popular ideas of the season. Epic said that in the first weekend alone, players redeemed an astonishing 116.4 million codes. New ones appeared regularly, hidden in Epic's posts, in trailers, and even in the middle of the world championship broadcast in September, when Epic revealed three codes live on air. One of them, fittingly, was WhoCrackedTheCode.

Even the Admin Panel itself has a sense of humour. Type a code that doesn't exist and, instead of a boring error message, it might reply with one of these:

One word to rule them all

Epic's co-founder, Mark Rein, has a tradition: before every new season, he posts a single word as a hint about what's coming. On 6 August 2026, two weeks before Override began, his word was: "Hackers". Looking back, it was the least cryptic clue of the lot.

How to pull it off

  1. Find the Admin Panel. It's in the top-right corner of the Battle Royale lobby.
  2. Only use official codes. Real Lobby Hacks come from Epic: its posts, trailers, live streams and in-game news. Codes are one-use, and they expire, so the ones in this book might not work any more.
  3. Never "pay" for a code. No website, video or person should ever ask for your password or money in exchange for a code. If they do, it's a scam. Tell a grown-up.
  4. Check for duplicates. If a code gives you a Sprite you already own, you get 10,000 Sprite Dust instead, so it's never wasted.
  5. Try decoding for fun. Use the hex decoder in this chapter to write secret messages to your friends. Can they crack 47 47?
