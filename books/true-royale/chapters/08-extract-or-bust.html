The prize only counts if you get home

You've had a brilliant match. You're carrying a Level 4 Spooky Dash Sprite, the storm is rolling in, and the nearest Extraction Site is on the far side of a hill crawling with enemies. You know the rule: if you're eliminated before you extract, the Sprite is gone, and all that levelling-up goes with it. So what do you do? Push on, or turn for home?

More than a hundred years ago, two teams of explorers faced exactly that question, except that the prize was a place nobody had ever stood, the storm was a real Antarctic blizzard, and the thing they risked losing was not a Sprite but their lives. This is the story of the race to the South Pole, and of the cruel truth that both teams discovered: reaching the prize was only half the job. Getting it home was the real test.

Two players, one lobby

In one corner stood Roald Amundsen, a Norwegian who had spent years in the Arctic learning from Inuit people, the Indigenous peoples of the far north, how to dress for brutal cold and how to drive teams of sledge dogs. In the other corner stood Captain Robert Falcon Scott of Britain's Royal Navy, who had already tried for the Pole once, in 1902, and had been forced to turn back.

Scott's expedition was huge and serious. It carried a dozen scientists, who planned to study rocks, weather, penguins and ice, as well as the team that would make the dash for the Pole. Amundsen's plan was simpler and more single-minded. In fact, Amundsen wasn't supposed to be going south at all. He had told everyone, including most of his own crew, that he was heading for the North Pole. Then, in 1909, two American explorers each claimed to have reached the North Pole first, and Amundsen quietly changed his plan. If the North Pole was taken, he would win the South one instead.

He kept the switch secret until his ship, the Fram, was already at sea. Then his brother Leon sent a telegram, a short message that travelled along electric wires and was printed out at the other end, to Scott. It reached Scott in Melbourne on 12 October 1910, and it said, in total: "Beg inform you Fram proceeding antarctic. Amundsen."

Five words, and the game had changed. Scott's carefully planned scientific expedition had suddenly become a race against a rival.

Fortnite file: the Override Console

Think of Amundsen's telegram as an Override. When you activate a console in Fortnite, you change the rules of the match for everyone, but your name appears around the console, so the whole lobby knows exactly where you've been. Amundsen did the same thing: his telegram changed the rules (now it was a race) but it also told his rival precisely where he was heading. He decided that was a price worth paying, because it was the honest thing to do.

Choosing a landing spot

Every Fortnite player knows that where you land shapes everything that follows. The two teams landed in very different places on the edge of the Ross Ice Shelf, a colossal sheet of floating ice roughly the size of France, which explorers of the time simply called "the Barrier".

Scott built his hut at Cape Evans, on solid rock on Ross Island, close to the route an earlier explorer, Ernest Shackleton, had already mapped. It was safe, and it was familiar. Amundsen took a bolder gamble. He built his hut, which he named Framheim ("home of the Fram"), at the Bay of Whales, right on top of the floating ice itself. Plenty of experts thought the ice there might break away and float out to sea. But Amundsen had studied old reports carefully and believed it was resting on hidden land underneath, and he was right. His reward was a starting point about 96 kilometres nearer to the Pole. High risk, high reward: a classic hot drop.

Five slots: picking a loadout

Here is a fact that would make any Fortnite player sit up: both teams sent exactly five people on the final push to the Pole. Five slots, just like your inventory. What each leader chose to fill them with turned out to matter enormously.

Amundsen's loadout was built around dogs. Sledge dogs are fast, they cope well with extreme cold, and they can live on meat that's carried along or found on the way. His men were also expert skiers. One of them, Olav Bjaaland, had been a national skiing champion back in Norway, and he was a skilled carpenter too: over the winter he planed the expedition's wooden sledges down until they weighed about a quarter of what they had before, without making them noticeably weaker. Lighter sledges meant faster dogs. Amundsen dressed his team in loose reindeer-skin furs copied from the Netsilik Inuit, whose way of life he had studied for two years. Animal fur, he had learned, keeps you warm without trapping sweat, and in the deep cold, sweat freezes.

Scott spread his bets more widely. He brought ponies, which had served Shackleton reasonably well, and three brand-new motor sledges, which were exciting, cutting-edge technology. He brought dogs too, but only for part of the journey. Unfortunately, the ponies struggled: they sank into soft snow, sweated through their coats, and needed heavy bales of food carried for them. The motor sledges broke down within the first eighty kilometres. From the foot of the glaciers onwards, Scott's men pulled their own sledges, a gruelling method called man-hauling, for more than three months.

Was Scott's loadout simply worse? It's tempting to say so, but historians warn against making it that simple. Scott's expedition was trying to do two things at once, reach the Pole and carry out a major scientific programme, and it collected over two thousand specimens, hundreds of them new to science. Amundsen had one objective and stripped away everything else. In Fortnite terms, Scott was trying to win the match and complete a stack of quests at the same time. That's not a foolish thing to attempt. It's just harder.

Supply drops: the depots

Neither team could possibly carry all its food and fuel from the start. Instead, during the summer before the big journey, both laid depots: stores of supplies left in advance along the route, ready to be picked up on the way to the Pole and, crucially, on the way back. They were the heals you stash before a fight, so that you'll find them when you need them most.

But a depot is only useful if you can find it again, in a white, featureless landscape where a blizzard can hide everything beyond your outstretched arm. Amundsen was obsessive about this. He placed a depot at every degree of latitude, and marked each one with a long line of flags stretching kilometres to either side of it, so that even a team that had drifted off course would cross the line and know which way to turn. Later, on the trip south, his men built snow-block cairns every few kilometres to guide them home. Scott's biggest store, One Ton Depot, was marked with what one of his men later called "just a cairn of snow" with a flag waving on a bamboo pole. As you'll see, that depot would matter terribly.

The storm closes in

The Antarctic summer is short, so both teams were racing against a closing window, much like a storm circle that's already shrinking before you've even jumped off the bus. Amundsen was so eager that he set off too early, in September 1911, and the temperature plunged to about minus 55 degrees Celsius. Several dogs died, and some of his men suffered frostbitten heels. So Amundsen did something that takes real nerve for a competitor: he turned round and went back to base to wait for warmer weather. Retreating felt like losing, but it was a precaution that probably saved the expedition.

He set off again in the second half of October with five men, four sledges and 52 dogs. Scott's main party left Cape Evans on 1 November.

Ninety degrees south

Amundsen's dogs carried his team across the Barrier at a steady pace, past depot after depot. They hauled themselves up a steep, unknown glacier through the mountains, which they climbed in just four days, then crossed a high, frozen plateau about three kilometres above sea level. On 14 December 1911, at about three in the afternoon, the drivers called out that their instruments said they had arrived. Amundsen later wrote that all five men gripped the flagpole together, "five weather-beaten, frost-bitten fists", to plant the Norwegian flag at the South Pole.

Then he admitted something wonderfully honest. All his life, he wrote, he had dreamed of the North Pole, "and here I was at the South Pole. Can anything more topsy-turvy be imagined?"

Before leaving, Amundsen pitched a small tent at the Pole and left two letters inside it. One was for Scott. The other was addressed to the King of Norway, describing what the team had achieved, and Amundsen explained exactly why he'd written it: "The way home was a long one, and so many things might happen to make it impossible for us to give an account of our expedition." He was planning for the possibility that he wouldn't extract, and making sure the prize wasn't lost even if he was. That's a Fortnite pro's mindset if ever there was one.

Amundsen's team at their tent at the South Pole, December 1911. Photo: Olav Bjaaland, 1911 (public domain), via Wikimedia Commons.

Scott's team, meanwhile, was still grinding towards the Pole on foot. On 16 January 1912, one of them spotted something dark on the white horizon. It was a black flag tied to a sledge runner: a marker Amundsen's men had left behind. Scott wrote in his diary that night: "The Norwegians have forestalled us and are first at the Pole. It is a terrible disappointment, and I am very sorry for my loyal companions."

They reached the Pole the next day, 17 January 1912, thirty-four days after Amundsen. Scott's diary entry is one of the most famous in the history of exploration: "Great God! this is an awful place and terrible enough for us to have laboured to it without the reward of priority." And then, a few lines later, he wrote a sentence that any player clutching an unextracted Sprite will understand: "Now for the run home and a desperate struggle. I wonder if we can do it."

Extraction

Amundsen's return journey went almost exactly to plan. His team followed their own flags and cairns from depot to depot, covering around 36 kilometres a day. At four o'clock in the morning on 25 January 1912, they arrived back at Framheim "with two sledges and eleven dogs; men and animals all hale and hearty". Here's the astonishing part: two years earlier, when he had first written down his secret plan, Amundsen had predicted the date of their return. The date he wrote was 25 January. They had been gone 99 days and travelled nearly 3,000 kilometres, and they came home on the very day he had planned. When a team member at the hut asked whether they'd reached the Pole, Amundsen reportedly answered, "Yes, of course; otherwise you would hardly have seen us again." Extracted.

Scott's team was not so fortunate, and it's important to tell what happened to them truthfully and with respect, because they were real people.

On the way back, the weather turned against them. Scientists who studied the records many years later found that the March of 1912 was far colder than usual on the Barrier, colder than almost any March measured there since. Edgar Evans, the strongest man in the team, became ill after a fall and died near the bottom of the glacier. Lawrence Oates's feet were so badly frozen that he could barely walk. Believing he was slowing his friends down, he stepped out of the tent into a blizzard, saying, "I am just going outside and may be some time." He was never seen again. Scott called him "a brave soul".

Scott, Edward Wilson and Henry Bowers kept going until a storm trapped them in their tent, about 20 kilometres short of One Ton Depot, the big store of food and fuel that might have saved them. For days the wind never stopped. Scott's last diary entry is dated 29 March 1912. Eight months later, a search party found the tent. Inside were Scott's diaries, letters, photographs and something remarkable: about 14 kilograms of rocks, which the exhausted men had refused to throw away, even as they struggled to pull their sledge.

Those rocks contained fossils of an ancient fern-like plant called Glossopteris. The same fossils are found in Australia, India, Africa and South America, and they helped scientists prove something extraordinary: that hundreds of millions of years ago, Antarctica and Australia were joined together in one giant supercontinent, called Gondwana. Scott's team never extracted themselves. But they carried their prize, their science, almost all the way, and in the end it made it home without them. One of the search party, a Norwegian ski expert called Tryggve Gran who had been part of Scott's team, wore Scott's own skis on the long trek back to base, so that, as he put it, at least Scott's skis would complete the journey.

News from Hobart

How did the world find out who had won? Through Australia. On 7 March 1912, the Fram sailed up the Derwent River into Hobart, and Amundsen came ashore and sent coded telegrams home, so that the news would appear first in the newspapers that had paid for his story. For a few confusing days some newspapers even reported that Scott had won. Before he left, Amundsen gave 21 of his dogs to the Australian explorer Douglas Mawson, whose expedition was exploring a different part of Antarctica at the same time.

There were Australians in Scott's team too. Two of his geologists, Frank Debenham (born in Bowral, New South Wales) and Griffith Taylor, had trained at the University of Sydney.

What the game teaches, and what it doesn't

Fortnite's extraction rule is a game designer's idea, but it captures something deeply true about exploration, and about plenty of other things in life: finishing well matters as much as starting brilliantly. Amundsen understood that the Pole was only the halfway point. He planned his depots, his flags and his return date as carefully as his route out.

He also summed up his whole approach in one famous sentence, which might be the best piece of Fortnite advice ever written by someone who'd never heard of Fortnite:

"Victory awaits him who has everything in order: luck, people call it. Defeat is certain for him who has neglected to take the necessary precautions in time; this is called bad luck."

Roald Amundsen, The South Pole (1912)

But there's one big difference between a game and history. When you're eliminated in Fortnite, you lose a Sprite and you're back in the lobby thirty seconds later. Real explorers didn't get a respawn. That's why the people in this story are remembered with such respect, the winners for their planning, and Scott's team for their courage, their loyalty to each other, and the science they carried until the very end.

Storm circle: the camp that kept shrinking

Three years later, another famous explorer lived through the ultimate shrinking safe zone. In 1915, Ernest Shackleton's ship, the Endurance, was trapped and crushed by sea ice, and its 28 men had to camp on the frozen ocean itself. Their first ice floe, a flat, floating slab of ice, was about a mile (1.6 kilometres) across. As the ice drifted north it cracked and split, again and again, until their camp was, in Shackleton's words, "a triangular raft of ice, the three sides measuring, roughly, 90, 100, and 120 yds", about the size of a football pitch.

Each man was allowed to keep just two pounds (about 900 grams) of personal belongings: the tightest inventory limit imaginable. "Sovereigns were thrown away and photographs were kept," Shackleton wrote. In other words, the men threw away gold coins and kept pictures of their families. Every one of the 28 men eventually made it home, thanks to an astonishing journey in small open boats. The expedition's photographer, Frank Hurley, was born in Glebe, in Sydney. In 2022, explorers found the wreck of the Endurance three kilometres down on the floor of the Weddell Sea, remarkably well preserved.

Myth busters

"Amundsen beat Scott by a month." Close, but it was 34 days, nearly five weeks.

"Scott died 11 miles from safety." He was 11 nautical miles (about 20 kilometres) from a depot of food and fuel, not from his base, which was more than 250 kilometres further on.

"Scott lost because he made silly choices." Too simple. His choices mattered, but so did an unusually cold March, illness, and fuel that had leaked out of the cans at his depots. Amundsen himself would have called that bad luck catching up with less careful planning.
