True story #5
The closest world final ever
2 PTS

The Fortnite Global Championship is the biggest event in competitive Fortnite: the world cup of the game. In 2026 it was held over two days, 26 and 27 September, in a sold-out arena in the city of Antwerp, in Belgium. Fifty duos, the best pairs of players from every region on Earth, had qualified to play twelve matches for a share of two million US dollars. For fans in Sydney, the broadcast started at around midnight, and plenty of them stayed up anyway.

Those who did witnessed something extraordinary. After twelve matches and hundreds of eliminations, the top three teams were separated by just four points. And the world title was decided by two.

How the scoring works

To understand what happened, you need to know one thing about tournament Fortnite: winning a match isn't everything. Teams earn points for how high they finish in every game, called placement points, plus points for every elimination. The scores are cumulative, which means they're added up across all twelve games, and the team with the highest total at the end is the champion.

That completely changes the way the best players think. A team that wins one game in spectacular style and then gets knocked out early in the next three can easily be overtaken by a team that never wins at all, but finishes in the top ten every single time. In other words, the format rewards consistency far more than occasional brilliance.

Want proof? At the 2026 Global Championship, Koyota and yuma, from the Japanese team ZETA DIVISION, won two of the twelve matches, and finished eighth. Another duo, Curve and Higgs, also won two, and finished tenth. The team that led after the first day had won none. And the team that eventually became world champions won just one.

Day one: the leaders who never won

After the first six matches, the leaders were vic0, from Austria, and Malibuca, with 304 points. Remarkably, they hadn't won a single game; they had simply finished near the top of almost every match, steadily collecting points like a squirrel stockpiling nuts for winter.

In fourth place, 74 points behind, sat SwizzY and Pixie, from the team HavoK by Vitality. SwizzY, aged 20, had already won the Global Championship the year before, in a team of three. Pixie, 19, had come second at the same event, on a different team, and had been waiting a whole year for another chance. A few places further back, in sixth, were shxrk, from Romania, and t3eny, from Slovenia. They had won the final match of the day. shxrk was fifteen years old, the youngest player in the entire tournament.

Day two: the long chase

On the second day, vic0 and Malibuca won the first game and stretched their lead even further. With just two matches left, they were 78 points clear, and most people watching assumed the contest was over. SwizzY and Pixie's team later said that, by its own calculations, their chances of winning at the start of the final day had been about half of one per cent.

But SwizzY and Pixie had won Game 10, their only Victory Royale of the whole weekend, and they had kept piling up points. In Game 11, they finished fourth with four eliminations, worth 64 points. The leaders, meanwhile, were knocked out early, in 34th place, with no eliminations at all. Suddenly, going into the twelfth and final match, the leaderboard read:

Game 12

The final match was chaos. vic0 and Malibuca, needing only a respectable finish, were eliminated in 43rd place, once again without a single elimination, and their enormous lead had evaporated in the space of two games.

SwizzY and Pixie played carefully, picked up two eliminations, and were knocked out in 12th place. That gave them 36 more points: a total of 525. After that, there was nothing they could do except watch, and hope.

Because shxrk and t3eny were still alive, and they were on fire. Needing a huge score, the fifteen-year-old and t3eny took fight after fight, collecting nine eliminations, including knocking out a pair of former world champions. Their total climbed and climbed towards the top of the leaderboard. The gaming news site Hotspawn wrote that the title was "decided by five minutes in the last game", with "multiple teams looking like the victors before just falling too early." Then t3eny went down, and moments later, so did shxrk. They finished sixth in the match, with 76 points. Their final total: 523.

Two points short. Out of more than five hundred.

SwizzY and Pixie were world champions. SwizzY became the first player ever to win the Global Championship two years in a row. Pixie, second the year before, finally had the title that had slipped away twelve months earlier. Between them they took home 400,000 US dollars, the championship trophy and a special in-game pickaxe called the Axe of Champions. Their average finishing position across all twelve games was about 13th: they won the world title without ever dominating a single day, simply by never having a disaster.

The fifteen-year-old

It would be easy to overlook the team that finished second, but that would be a mistake. shxrk and t3eny had arrived as one of the youngest pairs in the tournament and come within two points of the biggest prize in the game, earning 240,000 dollars along the way. Afterwards, shxrk's own post simply stated the result: second place at the world championship. A couple of weeks later, on 10 October, tournament results showed shxrk finishing first in Europe's Solo Fast Track, a qualifying event that led straight to to the solo finals at the end of October. Showing that kind of composure, so soon after such a narrow defeat, is impressive at any age.

And the other heartbroken team, vic0 and Malibuca? Their story is so useful to any Fortnite player that it gets its own chapter later in this book. All you need to know for now is that it involves the storm.

Why don't the pros get Sprites?

You might have noticed that this chapter doesn't mention Sprites, Overrides or Loot Hacks. That's not an accident. When the season began, Sprites appeared in Ranked matches, the mode used for competitive play, but Epic had switched their powers off "to preserve competitive integrity", meaning to keep things fair. A week later, Epic removed Sprites from Ranked completely, while it worked out whether collecting them "makes sense" there. Override Consoles and Loot Hacks aren't part of Ranked either, and Epic even took Miniguns out of competitive play, it said, "to keep things from getting too spray-happy".

The reason is fairness. When there's two million dollars at stake, nobody wants the result to depend on who happened to find a lucky Mythic Sprite or the right cartridge in a console. So the pros play a cleaner, more predictable version of the game, where skill decides almost everything.

How to pull it off

You're probably not playing for 400,000 dollars this weekend. But the champions' approach works in Ranked, in Arena-style events and in ordinary matches too.

  1. Every match counts. In Ranked, your result depends on where you finish as well as how many players you eliminate. A careful top-ten finish beats an early exit after one exciting fight.
  2. Don't throw away a good position. If you're doing well, avoid risky fights you don't need. Bank the points.
  3. Know the situation. Pros always know whether they need eliminations or survival. In the last game, shxrk and t3eny needed eliminations, so they took risks. SwizzY and Pixie needed a safe finish, so they didn't. Ask yourself: what do I need from this match?
  4. Stay calm after a bad game. Nobody plays perfectly for twelve matches. Composure means not letting one bad game turn into three.
