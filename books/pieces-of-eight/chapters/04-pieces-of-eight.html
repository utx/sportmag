Chapter 4
Real pirates, real treasure

Luffy is made up. Treasure Hunters is made up. But pirates were real, and their true stories are wilder than most video games. So let's drop anchor in the real past for a while.

There have been pirates for as long as there have been ships worth robbing. But the swashbuckling pirates you picture (tricorn hats, cutlasses, skull-and-crossbones flags) come from one stretch of history, roughly the 1650s to the 1730s, called the Golden Age of Piracy. It mostly happened in the Caribbean and along the coasts of the Americas and Africa, where ships stuffed with silver and sugar crossed the Atlantic, just waiting to be plundered.

Pieces of eight

When pirates did grab treasure, it was usually coins, and the most famous coin was the Spanish silver dollar. It was worth eight smaller coins called reales, so English sailors called it a piece of eight.

Pieces of eight were the most trusted money on Earth. Each one always held the same amount of silver, so people accepted them everywhere, from Mexico to China: the world's first truly global money. Many historians think the dollar sign, $, began as a drawing of the two ribbon-wrapped pillars on the back of the coin. And the most famous parrot in literature, Captain Flint from Treasure Island, spends half the book screeching "Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!" Which is how this book got its name.

The surprising truth, though, is that most pirate loot wasn't treasure at all. Pirates mostly stole useful things: food, water, rope, sails, medicine and often the ship itself. You can't eat a gold coin in the middle of the ocean.

The Sydney coin with a hole in it

Pieces of eight have a very Sydney story. In the early 1800s, the colony of New South Wales hardly had any coins, because ships kept carrying the money away. So in 1812, Governor Lachlan Macquarie (yes, the Macquarie of Macquarie Street) bought 40,000 Spanish dollars. Then he had a brilliant, slightly bonkers idea. He hired William Henshall, a convict who'd been sent to Australia for forging money, and had him punch the middle out of every single coin.

The ring became the holey dollar and the little middle disc became the dump, and together they were worth more than the original coin. That was the ingenious part: nobody could make a profit by shipping them out of the colony, so the money finally stayed put. Only a few hundred holey dollars survive today, and they're worth a fortune. Look closely at the logo of Macquarie Group, a big Sydney bank, and you'll spot one.

Pirate, privateer or buccaneer?

A privateer was a captain with a government's official permission to attack an enemy country's ships in wartime. To one side, a hero. To the other, a pirate with paperwork. Buccaneers were the wild sea-raiders of the 1600s Caribbean, named after the boucan, a wooden frame the local Taíno people used for smoking meat. (Another Taíno word, barbacoa, gave us "barbecue".)

Blackbeard was a pirate for only about two years, but he became the most notorious of all, because he was a genius at looking scary. A book from 1724 says he went into battle with slow-burning fuses tucked under his hat, so smoke curled around his enormous black beard. Many ships surrendered the moment they saw him. His flagship, the Queen Anne's Revenge, ran aground off North Carolina in 1718, and its wreck was found in 1996.

Anne Bonny and Mary Read sailed with "Calico Jack" Rackham's crew around Jamaica in 1720. When the navy finally caught the ship, the story goes, the two women were the last ones still fighting on deck while the men hid below.

Ching Shih, in an imaginative illustration from 1836. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

But the most powerful pirate in history might have been Ching Shih (also called Zheng Yi Sao), who led hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of pirates in the South China Sea in the early 1800s. Warships tried again and again to defeat her fleets. In 1810, she simply made a deal: her pirates would stop, and in return they'd be pardoned. She kept her fortune, ran a business and died of old age. Undefeated.

And Captain Kidd? He really did bury treasure, on an island near New York in 1699, hoping to bargain for his freedom. It was dug up as evidence, and Kidd was hanged in 1701. But people never stopped believing he'd hidden more, and for three hundred years treasure hunters have dug up beaches from Canada to Vietnam looking for it. Nobody has found a single coin.

Real Pirates: the only proven pirate treasure ship

In April 1717, the pirate ship Whydah was smashed to pieces by a storm off Cape Cod, in America, with its stolen treasure on board. In 1984, an explorer found the wreck, and in 1985 his divers brought up the ship's bell. When they cleaned it, they read the words THE WHYDAH GALLY 1716: the first pirate shipwreck ever found and proven beyond doubt. Thousands of coins have come up since, including pieces of eight.

Pirates had rules. Strict ones.

Surprisingly, many pirate crews were run like a democracy. The crew voted for their captain (and could vote him out), signed written rules before every voyage, and shared the loot by agreement, with an elected quartermaster making sure it was fair. The rules of the pirate captain Bartholomew Roberts, printed in 1724, included: every man gets a vote; no gambling for money on board; the musicians get Sundays off; and lights out at eight o'clock. Yes, pirates had a bedtime.

Break the rules, and you might be marooned: left alone on a deserted island with a bottle of water and a pistol.

The map that started it all: Robert Louis Stevenson's map of Treasure Island, 1883. Find the red crosses. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The X-marks-the-spot treasure map comes from Robert Louis Stevenson, who drew an imaginary island to entertain his stepson on a rainy holiday, then wrote a whole novel to go with it: Treasure Island (1883). That one book gave us nearly every pirate cliché: the map, the one-legged cook, the parrot, the chest of gold. And the growly pirate voice? That was actor Robert Newton, who played Long John Silver in Disney's 1950 film and rolled his R's in a wildly exaggerated version of his West Country English accent. "Arrr!" was born, and pirates have sounded like him ever since. Real pirates would have sounded like ordinary sailors. Somebody embellished the story, and the embellishment won.
