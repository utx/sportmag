Chapter 4
Real pirates, real treasure

Luffy and his crew are made up. Treasure Hunters is made up. But pirates were real, and their true stories are wilder than most video games. So before we meet Fortnite's crews, let's drop anchor in the real past for a while.

There have been pirates for as long as there have been ships worth robbing: in ancient Greece, in the Viking age and in the seas around China. But the swashbuckling pirates you picture (tricorn hats, cutlasses, skull-and-crossbones flags) come from one particular stretch of time, roughly from the 1650s to the 1730s. Historians call it the Golden Age of Piracy. Most of it happened in the Caribbean Sea and along the coasts of the Americas and Africa, where Spanish, British and French ships carried silver, sugar and all sorts of valuable cargo across the Atlantic Ocean, just waiting to be plundered.

What was pirate treasure, really?

When pirates did capture gold and silver, their booty was usually in the form of coins. The most famous was the Spanish silver dollar, which was worth eight smaller coins called reales. Its proper Spanish name was real de a ocho, a "real of eight", and English sailors called it a piece of eight.

Pieces of eight were the most trusted money on Earth. Spain made millions of them from silver dug out of mines in the Americas, and because each coin always held the same amount of silver, people accepted them everywhere: in Europe, in Africa, in India and in China. They were, in effect, the world's first global currency. They were still legal money in the United States until 1857, and many historians think the dollar sign, $, began as a drawing of the two pillars wrapped in ribbons on the back of the coin.

And yes: the most famous parrot in literature, Captain Flint, who sits on Long John Silver's shoulder in Treasure Island, spends half the book screeching "Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!" Which is how this book got its name.

But here's the surprising truth: most pirate "treasure" wasn't treasure at all. Pirates spent far more time stealing ordinary, useful things: food, fresh water, rope, sails, tools, medicine, cloth, barrels of sugar and, often, the ship itself. A pirate couldn't eat a gold coin in the middle of the ocean.

The Sydney coin with a hole in it

Pieces of eight have a special connection to Sydney. In the early 1800s, the young colony of New South Wales had a big problem: it hardly had any coins. Ships kept carrying money away, and people had to pay each other with promissory notes, goods and even rum.

So in 1812, Governor Lachlan Macquarie (yes, the Macquarie of Macquarie Street) bought 40,000 Spanish dollars, which arrived in Sydney on 26 November 1812. Then he had a brilliant, slightly bonkers idea. He hired William Henshall, a convict who had been transported to Australia for forgery (making fake money!), and had him punch the middle out of every single coin.

The outer ring became the holey dollar, worth five shillings. The little disc from the middle, stamped with a crown, became the dump, worth one shilling and threepence. Together, the two pieces were worth more than the original coin, which was the clever part: nobody could make a profit by shipping them out of the colony, so the money finally stayed in New South Wales. They went into use in 1814 and were withdrawn in 1829. Only a few hundred holey dollars survive today, and they're worth a fortune to collectors. Look closely at the logo of a certain big Sydney bank, Macquarie Group, and you'll see a holey dollar.

Pirate, privateer or buccaneer?

Not everyone who attacked ships called themselves a pirate. A privateer was a sea captain given official permission by a government, in a document called a letter of marque, to attack the ships of an enemy country during a war. To one side, a privateer was a hero. To the other, a privateer was just a pirate with paperwork.

Buccaneers were the wild sailors of the 1600s Caribbean, many of whom started out as hunters on the island of Hispaniola. They cooked their meat on a wooden frame that the local Taíno people called a boucan, which is where the word comes from. (The Taíno word barbacoa gave us another word you know: barbecue.)

Blackbeard: the master of looking scary

Blackbeard, in an engraving from A General History of the Pyrates, 1724. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Edward Teach (or Thatch: even his name is a mystery) was a pirate for only about two years, but he became the most notorious of them all. His great talent was his image. A book written six years after his death, A General History of the Pyrates (1724), says he went into battle with lit, slow-burning fuses tucked under his hat, so that smoke curled around his huge black beard and made him look like a demon. Whether or not every detail is true, it worked: many ships surrendered the moment they saw his flag. His flagship, the Queen Anne's Revenge, ran aground off North Carolina in 1718, and its wreck was found in 1996. Divers have been bringing up its cannons ever since. Blackbeard himself was caught by the British navy later in 1718.

Anne Bonny and Mary Read: the pirates nobody expected

In 1720, a pirate ship led by "Calico Jack" Rackham (nicknamed for his colourful cotton clothes) prowled the waters around Jamaica. Two of its toughest crew members were women: Anne Bonny and Mary Read, who wore sailors' clothes and, according to the 1724 book, fought more fiercely than any man aboard. When the navy finally caught the ship off Jamaica in October 1720, the story goes that the two women were the last ones still fighting on deck, while the men hid below. Their story has been retold in books, films and games ever since.

Ching Shih: the pirate who won

Ching Shih, in an imaginative illustration from 1836. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The most powerful pirate in history may not have been Blackbeard at all. In the early 1800s, Ching Shih (also known as Zheng Yi Sao) led an enormous alliance of pirate fleets in the South China Sea: hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of pirates, far more than any Caribbean captain ever commanded. She ran it with strict rules, and Chinese and Portuguese warships tried again and again to defeat her fleets without ever finishing the job. In 1810, she negotiated a deal with the Chinese government: her pirates would stop, and in return they would be pardoned. She kept her fortune, ran a business in the city of Canton, and died in 1844, aged about 68. A pirate who retired rich and died of old age was a rare thing indeed.

Captain Kidd: the treasure was real (some of it)

William Kidd began as a privateer, hired to hunt pirates in the Indian Ocean. Instead, he ended up being accused of piracy himself. Sailing home to explain himself in 1699, he stopped at Gardiners Island, near New York, and (with the owner's permission) buried a chest of gold, silver and jewels there, probably hoping to use it to bargain for his freedom. It didn't work. The governor had the treasure dug up as evidence, and Kidd was sent to London and hanged in 1701. But people never stopped believing he'd hidden more treasure somewhere. For three hundred years, treasure hunters have dug up beaches and islands from Canada to Vietnam looking for "Captain Kidd's gold". Nobody has found any.

The only pirate treasure ever proven

On 26 April 1717, a pirate ship called the Whydah, captained by "Black Sam" Bellamy, was caught in a ferocious storm off Cape Cod, in America, and smashed to pieces, with its stolen treasure still on board. For more than 250 years it lay under the sand. Then, in 1984, an explorer named Barry Clifford found the wreck. In 1985 his divers brought up the ship's bell, and when they cleaned it, they read the words THE WHYDAH GALLY 1716. It was the first pirate shipwreck ever to be found and proven beyond doubt. Thousands of coins, including pieces of eight, have been recovered from it since.

Pirates had rules. Strict ones.

Here's something that surprises everyone: in some ways, pirate ships were more fair than almost anywhere else in the 1700s. Most pirate crews were run as a kind of democracy. The crew voted to choose their captain, and they could vote him out again. Before a voyage, everyone signed a set of written rules called the articles, which said how the loot would be shared. Usually the captain got about two shares, and everyone else got one. An officer called the quartermaster made sure the sharing was fair.

The articles of the pirate captain Bartholomew Roberts, printed in 1724, survive. They include:

  • Every man has a vote in important decisions.
  • Lights and candles out at eight o'clock at night. (Yes, pirates had a bedtime.)
  • No gambling for money on board.
  • The musicians get Sundays off.

Break the articles, and the punishment could be harsh. The worst was being marooned: left alone on a deserted island with a bottle of water and a pistol.

The map that started it all: Robert Louis Stevenson's map of Treasure Island, 1883. Find the red crosses. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The idea of an X marking the spot comes from Robert Louis Stevenson. He drew a map of an imaginary island to entertain his stepson on a rainy holiday, then wrote a whole novel to go with it: Treasure Island, published in 1883. That one book gave us almost every pirate cliché there is: the treasure map, the one-legged cook, the parrot, the chest of gold. And the pirate voice? That came from Robert Newton, the actor who played Long John Silver in Disney's 1950 film. He grew up in the south-west of England, and he rolled his R's and growled "Arrr!" in an exaggerated version of the local accent. Audiences loved it so much that pirates have sounded like him ever since.
