Never watched One Piece? No problem. By the end of this chapter, you'll be an expert. Already a fan? Count how many of these you knew.

One Piece in one page

One Piece is a Japanese manga (comic) by Eiichiro Oda. It started in 1997 and it's still going, with more than a hundred volumes, over a thousand anime episodes, films, games and a live-action TV show. It has sold more than 500 million copies, and in 2015 it set a Guinness World Record for the most copies of a comic series ever published by one author. In other words: colossal.

The story is set in a world that's mostly ocean. Long ago, the legendary Gol D. Roger, King of the Pirates, announced that he'd left all his treasure in one place at the far end of the world. He called it the One Piece, and ever since, thousands of pirates have gone looking for it.

One of them is Monkey D. Luffy, a cheerful, endlessly hungry teenager who wants to find the One Piece and become the next King of the Pirates. Along the way, he collects a crew of lovable misfits, each chasing a dream of their own: the Straw Hat Pirates, named after the battered hat he always wears.

And what actually is the One Piece? Nobody knows! The story isn't finished, and Oda hasn't told anyone. Which makes it the perfect theme for a treasure hunt.

The Devil Fruit rule

In One Piece, rare, strange fruits called Devil Fruits give you a superpower when you eat them, but there's a terrible price: you can never swim again. Fall into the sea and you sink like a stone. For a pirate, that's awkward.

Luffy ate the Gum-Gum Fruit as a boy, and now his whole body is rubber. He can stretch his arms across a room and bounce cannonballs off his belly. That stretchy punch in the trailer? That's his famous "Gum-Gum Pistol".

Before you watch One Piece

The anime is brilliant, funny and full of heart, but it's made for teenagers and some battles are intense. Ask a grown-up first, and maybe watch the first few episodes together. Fair warning: there are more than a thousand.

Real Pirates: the real "wanted" posters

Real governments put bounties on pirates, too. In 1718, the governor of Virginia, in America, issued an official proclamation offering £100 for capturing Blackbeard, a fortune at a time when an ordinary sailor might earn a pound or two a month. It worked: Blackbeard was caught that same year.
