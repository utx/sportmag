Chapter 13
Keep your cool

Everyone has had a match like this. You land, you lose the first fight, and you're back in the lobby in forty seconds. You queue again, angry. Same thing. Again. By the fourth match, you're playing worse and worse, your hands are tense, and you're grumpy with everyone around you.

Gamers have a word for this: tilt. It comes from old pinball machines, which stopped working if you shook them too hard. Being "on tilt" means you're so frustrated that it's making you play badly. And the most important skill in this chapter, maybe in the whole book, is learning to notice it and do something about it.

The tilt meter

Learn to spot the signs that you're heading into the hot zone:

  • You're pressing the buttons harder than usual.
  • You're rushing into fights you'd normally avoid.
  • You're blaming your teammates, the game, your controller or "lag".
  • You're queuing for the next match straight away, without even thinking about what happened.
  • You're not having fun any more.

If you notice two or more of these, you're on tilt. And here's the thing: playing more won't fix it. Tilted players make worse decisions, lose more, get more frustrated, and make even worse decisions. It's a spiral.

The reset routine

The cure is simple: stop, and reset. It works for professional players, and it'll work for you.

The reset routine

Some players also use a simple rule: three losses in a row, take a break. Not because three losses is bad (it happens to everyone), but because it's a good moment to check the tilt meter.

Mistakes are clues, not crimes

Every time you get eliminated, you've just been given a free lesson. Something went wrong, and now you can work out what. Were you in the open? Did you rotate late? Did you miss your shotgun shot? The player who asks "what can I learn?" gets better. The player who says "that was so unfair" stays the same.

Being resilient doesn't mean never feeling disappointed. Everyone does. It means feeling it, then letting it go, and coming back ready to learn. That's useful far beyond Fortnite: in sport, in music, at school, everywhere.

Remember: videos are highlights

Watching streamers and pro players can make you feel like everyone is amazing except you. But remember what you're watching. Videos show the best bits, picked out of hours of play. Those players have practised for years, often for many hours a day, and they lose plenty of matches too: they just don't put those in the highlights. Compare yourself with you last month, not with someone else's best clip.

Win well, lose well

Good sportsmanship isn't just for football fields. In Fortnite, it looks like this:

  • Say "GG" (good game) to opponents and teammates, win or lose.
  • Don't show off over players you've eliminated. Everybody gets eliminated sometimes, including you.
  • No rude messages, and no blaming teammates. Real people are on the other end.
  • Never cheat. Cheats and hacks get accounts banned, and they're often scams that steal accounts anyway. They also make the game worse for everyone.

Look after the player

The most important piece of equipment isn't your controller or your headphones. It's you. Players who look after themselves play better, and feel better too.

  • Take regular breaks. Every 20 minutes or so, look at something far away (out of a window is perfect) for 20 seconds. Your eyes will thank you.
  • Sit well. Sit back in your chair, with your feet on the floor and your screen at a comfortable distance.
  • Drink water, and eat proper food (not just snacks).
  • Sleep. Tired players have slower reactions. Stopping gaming a while before bed helps you sleep better.
  • Stick to the screen-time rules at home. They're there for good reasons, and the Island will still be there tomorrow.
  • Do other things. Sport, reading, drawing, friends. It keeps life interesting, and lots of skills (like teamwork and keeping calm) carry over both ways.

Stay safe online

  • Nobody is giving away free V-Bucks. If a website, video or message offers them, it's a scam.
  • Never share your password or account with anyone, not even a friend.
  • Ask a grown-up to help set up two-factor authentication (an extra security code) and parental controls.
  • Never spend money without asking a grown-up first.
  • If something online upsets you, tell a grown-up you trust. You won't be in trouble.

Try this drill: the good-mood finish

For one week, try to finish every gaming session on a good note: a win, a good fight, a fun match with friends, or just after a reset. Stop then, instead of carrying on until you're grumpy. You'll find you look forward to the next session much more, and keep a sense of perspective: it's a game, and games are meant to be fun.
