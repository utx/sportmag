Chapter 4
Pieces of eight

Here's a strange thing about a season called Treasure Hunters: the gold has gone. Completely. The shiny Gold Bars you used to collect and spend have been vaulted (taken out of the game), and the Vending Machines where you spent them have disappeared too.

So how do you get good loot now? You find it. This chapter explains how the treasure works on Crossbones Isle, and why that makes the whole game feel different.

No gold? No problem

Last season, a good player could land, grab some gold, and walk up to a Vending Machine to buy exactly what they wanted. Not any more. Now everything comes from chests, floor loot, bosses and other players. You use what you find.

Evolve Jake noticed two big effects. First, it's harder to find loot, and especially travel items (Chapter 2). Second, "it feels a lot more like an actual battle royale now." Everyone has to scramble and make do, which makes every chest exciting again.

There's some good news too. The characters on the island who offer services (for example, joining your team to help you fight) used to charge gold. Now they do it for free.

The chests come back

This is the biggest change of all. In Epic's words: "Chests now refill over time", so the hunt never runs dry. Open a chest early in the match, come back later, and you may find it full again. Evolve Jake spotted this in his late-game fights: chests that had obviously been opened already had fresh loot sitting next to them.

And sometimes a chest has an extra surprise. Evolve Jake explains that every chest has a small chance of a special reward: the screen shakes, a little animation plays, and you might win a bonus like a loading screen or a car wrap to keep. Don't count on it. It's rare! But it makes every single chest a tiny lucky dip.

Vaults and Rare Chests

Some of the best treasure is locked away. Hidden around the island are treasure vaults, rooms piled high with gold coins, ammo boxes and Rare Chests. You can't spend the gold, but TG Plays found that smashing the gold piles gives you metal for building. To open the door, you need a Vault Key. TG Plays found a Rare Vault Key, unlocked a vault, and came out with a pile of loot, including Zoro's Three Sword Style.

Inside a treasure vault. All that gold, and you can't spend any of it! (Frame: TG Plays)

Chests also feature in a quest: the Treasure Chest Hunt has five stages, starting with opening treasure chests. kevinsmak says that first stage is worth 10,000 XP.

The Golden Apple

The Golden Apple has been changed this season. According to Epic, it now gives 40 effective health instead of 50, its low-gravity effect (you jump higher after eating it) lasts a little longer, and it turns up in Produce Boxes more often. One guide adds that if your health is already full, the extra goes into your shield instead.

The Golden Apple. Jump high! (Image: Epic Games / Fortnite)

Treasure Hours

Every Sunday this season brings Treasure Hours. During Treasure Hours, Captain's weapons appear right from the start of every match, and drops are boosted. Epic hasn't said exactly what time they start, so check the news in the lobby on Sunday. (Remember the time-zone box in Chapter 0: in Sydney, the timing may surprise you.)

What about Sprite Dust?

There's still one thing you can spend: Sprite Dust, which you earn by playing and by finding Sprites (Chapter 5). You can spend it on things like Portable Extractors to save your Sprites.

Real treasure: pieces of eight (and Sydney's holey dollar)

"Pieces of eight!" squawks the parrot in Treasure Island. But what were they? They were real Spanish silver coins, called reales de a ocho, which means "eight reales". One coin was worth eight smaller coins called reales. Spain made millions of them from the silver it mined in the Americas, and they were used all over the world, from China to the Caribbean. Some people call them the world's first global money.

When people needed small change, it's said they sometimes cut the coin into eight wedges, like a pizza. Each wedge was a "bit", and two bits made a quarter. That's why some Americans still call a 25-cent coin "two bits"!

And here's where it gets local. In 1813, the colony of New South Wales, centred on Sydney, had a big problem: there weren't enough coins, and the ones it had kept leaving on ships. So Governor Lachlan Macquarie got hold of 40,000 Spanish dollars and had the middle punched out of every one. The ring became the "holey dollar", worth five shillings, and the little centre became the "dump", worth fifteen pence. They were the first coins ever made in Australia, and a coin with a hole in it wasn't much use anywhere else, so they stayed in the colony. Real ones are kept in museums today, including the National Museum of Australia in Canberra and the Australian National Maritime Museum at Darling Harbour in Sydney.

Do the maths

In old Australian money, there were 12 pence in a shilling. A Spanish dollar was worth about 5 shillings. After Macquarie's cut, the holey dollar was worth 5 shillings and the dump was worth 15 pence.

1. How many pence is 5 shillings?

2. How many pence were the holey dollar and the dump worth together?

3. So cutting one coin into two made it worth how many pence more? (Clever, Governor!)

Answers
1: 5 × 12 = 60 pence
2: 60 + 15 = 75 pence
3: 75 − 60 = 15 pence more
