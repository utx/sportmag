Chapter 8
Grids, scale and directions

"Enemy at D5! Pushing from the north-east!" If you've ever played squads, you've used a grid reference and a compass direction without even thinking about it. Your map is split into squares, each with a letter and a number, so everyone can say exactly where something is in a couple of words.

That simple idea, a grid with letters and numbers, is one of the most powerful tools ever invented. Sailors, pilots, game designers and the satnav in a car all depend on it.

The fly on the ceiling

There's a famous story that the French thinker René Descartes, who lived about 400 years ago, invented coordinates while lying in bed watching a fly crawl across his ceiling. He realised he could describe exactly where the fly was by saying how far it was from two walls. Nobody knows if the fly was real (it's probably a legend), but the idea definitely was. Graphs in maths still use his system, and they're called Cartesian coordinates in his honour.

Shrinking the world: scale

A map has to be much smaller than the real place, or it wouldn't fit in your pocket. The scale tells you how much smaller. On a map with a scale of "1 cm to 2 km", every centimetre on the paper stands for 2 kilometres of real ground.

We use a scale to put markers on the Fortnite map in our magazines. The game's own location data uses enormous numbers: a place might be tens of thousands of units from the middle. To turn those into a spot on a map picture 2,048 pixels wide, we multiply by a tiny number (0.00658) and then add 975 to move everything into the right position. Here's our real formula:

across the picture = 975 + (0.00658 × the game's "across" number)

The map that tells fibs

The Earth is round, but maps are flat. Try flattening the peel of an orange and you'll see the problem: something has to stretch or tear. In 1569, a map-maker named Gerardus Mercator made a world map that was perfect for sailors, because a straight line on it showed a constant compass direction. But to make that work, he had to stretch the parts near the North and South Poles, more and more the further you go.

The result: on Mercator's map, and on many world maps you'll still see today, Greenland looks about as big as Africa. In real life, Africa is about 14 times bigger. Even Australia is more than three times the size of Greenland, though on these maps they look much closer. A map can distort the truth without anybody meaning to.

Mapping this land

Long before any European map of Australia existed, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples had been finding their way across this continent for tens of thousands of years. In many Aboriginal cultures, knowledge of Country, including routes, landmarks and places to find water, is passed on through songs, stories and art.

In 1770, the British navigator James Cook sailed the ship Endeavour up the east coast, carefully drawing charts as he went. In late April, the Gweagal people watched the ship sail into Kamay, the bay Cook later named Botany Bay. The Endeavour stayed for eight days, and the meeting between the two peoples is still remembered and talked about today. About 30 years later, Matthew Flinders sailed all the way around the continent, and his book helped make the name "Australia" popular.
