Your brain is the best loot

Here's a secret that Epic Games will never put in the patch notes: Fortnite is stuffed with maths, science and history.

Every time you jump out of the Battle Bus, you're doing an experiment that a scientist called Galileo worked on 400 years ago. Every time the storm circle shrinks, you're watching a piece of geometry that puzzled the ancient Greeks. Your health bar is a fractions lesson. The Item Shop is a masterclass in persuasion. Even the falling blocks that squashed a town on the Island come from a game invented on a Soviet computer that couldn't draw pictures.

This book follows those clues out of the game and into the real world. Along the way you'll crack codes, count triangles, dance like a bee, and catch a famous villain getting his maths wrong. And you'll find puzzles everywhere, because a brain is like a Chug Jug: it only works if you use it.

Every puzzle has a number, like Puzzle 2.3 (chapter 2, puzzle 3), and a difficulty rating from one to three Chug Jugs. One Chug Jug is a warm-up. Two means you'll need to think it through. Three means full big-brain mode: take your time, draw a diagram, and don't panic if it takes a few goes.

All the answers are in How to Think It Through at the back. They don't just tell you the answer; they show you how to get there, because the way you think is more useful than any single answer. So have a proper go before you look. And if you get one wrong, that's great news: it's the fastest way to learn something.

Four rules for puzzle-solving

  1. Read the whole question. Then read it again. Most mistakes happen in the first five seconds.
  2. Write things down. Draw it, list it, make a table. Your brain is brilliant, but it's not a whiteboard.
  3. Check your answer makes sense. If you've worked out that a bee flies at 4,000 km/h, go back and look again.
  4. No calculator, unless a puzzle says you can use one. You'll be surprised what you can do on paper.

Meet Geno, who is always wrong

Geno

Very confident. Very loud. Not always right. Watch for him in the Spot the Flaw boxes.

This season's villain is Geno, the one who jammed a Cursed Cartridge into the Override console. His motto is "Perfection, embodied." He's sure he's never wrong about anything.

He is wrong about lots of things.

In every chapter, Geno makes an argument in a Spot the Flaw box. It will sound convincing. It might even sound clever. Your job is to find the flaw: the step where his thinking goes wrong. Learning to spot a bad argument is one of the most useful skills there is, in games, at school and everywhere else. (Especially in comment sections.)

Warm-up: Is Geno perfect?

Mathematicians had a special meaning for the word "perfect" more than 2,000 years before Geno came along. A perfect number is a number whose factors (the numbers that divide into it exactly, not counting the number itself) add up to the number itself.

The smallest one is 6. Its factors are 1, 2 and 3, because 6 ÷ 1, 6 ÷ 2 and 6 ÷ 3 all work with nothing left over. And 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. Perfect!

The Greek mathematician Euclid wrote about perfect numbers around 2,300 years ago. Since then, mathematicians have hunted for them with pencils, then with computers, and they've found only around fifty. The next one after 6 is 28. After that they get big very quickly.

Puzzle 0.1 · Number Crunch · 1 Chug Jug

Check that 28 is perfect. List all its factors (except 28 itself) and add them up.

Puzzle 0.2 · Number Crunch · 2 Chug Jugs

Is 12 a perfect number? Add up its factors (not counting 12) and find out. If the total is too big, mathematicians call the number abundant. If it's too small, they call it deficient. Which is 12?

Puzzle 0.3 · Number Crunch · 3 Chug Jugs

The third perfect number is between 400 and 500, and it's 16 × 31. Work out what it is, then prove it's perfect. (Hint: start with 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and keep doubling. Then try 31, 62…)

The small print

The Fortnite parts of this book describe Chapter 7, Season 4: Override, with the Fortnitemares update (version 42.30) on the Island, as it was on 12 October 2026. Fortnite changes all the time, so if something in the game is different, the game has changed.

The history, science and maths are real, and we've checked them against good sources (they're listed at the back). When historians or scientists disagree about something, or when a famous story might not be true, we say so. Some puzzles use made-up numbers to make the maths work; they always say "imagine" or "pretend" when they do.

A note for grown-ups

This is an unofficial Fortnite fan book that uses the game as a way into history, science, maths, reading and reasoning. There's no violence beyond a passing mention of cartoon "eliminations", and nothing in it asks anyone to spend money. Guest characters from other companies (such as the Override season's arcade and video-game crossovers) are mentioned by name only, with no pictures. The puzzles range from warm-ups to genuinely tricky, and every answer comes with worked reasoning at the back.
