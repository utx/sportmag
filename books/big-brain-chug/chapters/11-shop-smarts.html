Chapter 11
Money maths and persuasion

Every day at the same time, the Item Shop changes. New outfits, new emotes, a countdown timer, bright colours, big "NEW!" labels. It's exciting. It's also designed that way. Lots of clever people have worked hard to make you feel that you need something, right now, before it's gone.

This chapter isn't about telling you never to buy anything. It's about giving you two superpowers: the maths to work out what's good value, and the eyes to spot when someone is trying to persuade you. (And the most important rule of all: always ask a grown-up before spending a single V-Buck.)

What's the best value?

Bigger packs often look like better value, but you can only be sure if you work out the price of one. That's called the unit price: divide the total price by the number of things you get.

Spot the tricks

Adverts use the same handful of tricks again and again. Once you know them, you'll see them everywhere: on TV, on websites, in game shops and on the cereal box at breakfast.

TrickWhat it sounds likeWhat it's doing
Hurry up!"Only 3 hours left!" "Last chance!"Making you decide quickly, before you have time to think.
Nearly gone"Rare!" "Limited edition!"Using scarcity to make something feel more precious.
Everyone's doing it"Everyone's got one!"Making you worry about being left out. (This is called the bandwagon trick.)
Big promises"The best ever!" "You'll never lose again!"Exaggerating, or dressing up an opinion as a fact.
The fake bargain"Was 2,000, NOW 1,500!"Showing a big "before" price so the real price feels small. Was it ever really 2,000?

The craziest shopping spree in history

Tulips first came to Western Europe from the Ottoman Empire (modern-day Turkey) in the 1500s, and people in the Dutch Republic (today's Netherlands) fell in love with them. The most wanted of all were rare tulips with flame-like stripes on their petals. Nobody knew how to grow those stripes on purpose; they just appeared, which made those bulbs scarce.

In the 1630s, prices for the rarest bulbs climbed and climbed. People bought bulbs hoping to sell them for even more. Then, in February 1637, buyers suddenly stopped buying, and prices crashed. It's remembered as tulip mania, and for nearly 400 years people have told wild stories about it: single bulbs swapped for whole houses, rich families ruined overnight, a whole country gone bankrupt over flowers.

But here's the twist. When the historian Anne Goldgar studied the actual records from the time (contracts, court cases, letters), she found that very few people seem to have been ruined, and the country carried on as normal. Many of the most dramatic stories came from songs and pamphlets written at the time to make fun of tulip traders. It's a great lesson in checking where a story came from.

The secret of the stripes

Those valuable stripes were caused by a virus, spread from plant to plant by tiny insects called aphids. Scientist Dorothy Cayley worked this out in the 1920s. The virus also made the bulbs weaker over time, so the most expensive tulips in the world were actually sick!

Before you spend a single V-Buck

  • Always ask a grown-up first. Every time.
  • Sleep on it. If you still want it tomorrow, that's a good sign. If you've forgotten about it, you just saved your money.
  • Remember the shop rotates. Most items come back eventually, so you rarely need to rush.
  • There are no free V-Bucks. Any website or video that promises them is a scam.
