Chapter 6
Algorithms and rules

Fly over the north of the Island and you'll spot an island shaped like a hungry yellow circle with a slice missing. It's one of several arcade-themed spots from the Override season, built in honour of Pac-Man, the 1980 arcade game made by the Japanese company Namco (it belongs to them, so no pictures here). At night, robotic ghosts roam around these islands, and if you defeat one, you might get the Blinky Sprite, which hides you for a moment whenever you take damage.

In the original game, four ghosts chase you around a maze. They seem to have personalities: one is relentless, one is sneaky, one is unpredictable, and one seems a bit hopeless. But here's the secret. None of them can think. Each one just follows a simple set of rules. And a set of rules like that has a special name.

Four ghosts, four rules

The ghosts' original Japanese names describe their jobs. Here's what each one does in the original arcade game:

GhostJapanese name means…Its rule
Blinky (red)"Chaser"Head for the exact square where the player is right now.
Pinky (pink)"Ambusher"Head for the square four spaces in front of the player, to cut them off.
Inky (blue)"Fickle" (moody)Use a tricky rule that depends on where both the player AND Blinky are. Hard to predict!
Clyde (orange)"Playing dumb"Chase the player when far away, but when he gets close, wander off to his corner.

That's it. Four simple rules, and together they make a chase that feels clever and alive. A set of step-by-step rules like this is called an algorithm, and algorithms are everywhere. A recipe is an algorithm. So are the instructions for building LEGO, the way a satnav finds a route, and every single thing that happens inside Fortnite.

The pizza story: true or false?

The most famous story about Pac-Man is that its designer, Toru Iwatani, got the idea when he took a slice from a pizza and looked at what was left. Iwatani himself has told the story. But in an interview in 1986, he admitted it was only "half true": the shape also came from rounding off the Japanese written character for "mouth", which is a square. When even the inventor says a story is "half true", it's worth being a little sceptical about the stories you hear!

Where the word comes from

About 1,200 years ago, in the city of Baghdad, there was a famous library and research centre called the House of Wisdom. One of its scholars was Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a brilliant mathematician. He wrote a book explaining how to do calculations with the numbers 0 to 9 that had come from India, the same digits we use today. When his book was translated into Latin, his name was written as Algoritmi. That's where the word algorithm comes from!

Al-Khwarizmi wrote another famous book, about solving puzzles with unknown numbers. Its title included the Arabic word al-jabr, meaning "restoring" or "putting back together". You might know it better as algebra.

The first programmer

In the 1830s and 1840s, an English inventor named Charles Babbage designed a giant mechanical calculating machine called the Analytical Engine. It would have been powered by steam and full of brass gears. It was never finished. But a young mathematician named Ada Lovelace understood it better than almost anyone.

In 1843, she published long notes about the machine. In one of them, now called Note G, she set out step by step how the Engine could work out a tricky list of numbers called Bernoulli numbers, including steps that repeat, which programmers today call a loop. It's often called the first computer program ever published, more than 100 years before modern computers existed. (Historians still argue about how much of the work was hers and how much was Babbage's.) She also guessed that one day such machines might even compose music. She was right.

Why is a mistake called a "bug"?

On 9 September 1947, engineers working on the Harvard Mark II computer (where the famous programmer Grace Hopper worked) found a real moth stuck inside the machine. They taped it into their logbook with the note: "First actual case of bug being found." Engineers had already been calling faults "bugs" for years (the inventor Thomas Edison used the word in 1878), but the moth made the joke famous. Fixing mistakes in a program is still called debugging.

Program the Robot Llama

Time to write your own algorithms. The Robot Llama lives on the grid below. Squares are named by their column letter and row number, so the bottom-left square is A1. The Robot Llama always starts on A1, facing north (up the page), and it understands only three commands:

  • F: move forward one square.
  • L: turn left, a quarter turn, on the spot.
  • R: turn right, a quarter turn, on the spot.
