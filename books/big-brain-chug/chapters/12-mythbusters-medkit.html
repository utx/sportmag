Chapter 12
Evidence and fair tests

The whole Fortnitemares event began when Geno slotted a Cursed Cartridge into the Override console. A game cartridge! Before games were downloaded, they came on plastic cartridges that you pushed into the console. And if a cartridge didn't work, almost every kid in the world knew the fix: pull it out, blow into it, and push it back in.

Did it actually work? Millions of people were sure it did. That's the problem with being sure. In this chapter, you'll learn how scientists find out what's really true, and you'll bust some famous myths along the way.

Word Watch

Mythnoun
A story or belief that lots of people think is true, but isn't. (It can also mean an ancient story about gods and heroes.)
Evidencenoun
Facts or information that show whether something is true.
Coincidencenoun
When two things happen at the same time by chance, even though one didn't cause the other.
Scepticaladjective
Not believing something straight away; wanting to see the evidence first.

Myth 1: Blowing into cartridges

Here's what repair experts say now: blowing into a cartridge probably did very little. It might have shifted a bit of dust. But your breath is damp, and moisture on metal contacts can make them corrode (go rusty and grotty) over time, which makes things worse. So why did it seem to work? The most likely answer is that taking the cartridge out and pushing it back in properly fixed the connection. The blowing just happened at the same time. That's a coincidence, and it fooled a whole generation.

The lesson: just because B happened after A doesn't mean A caused B.

Myth 2: My lucky outfit

Lots of players have a "lucky" outfit. You wore it, you got a Victory Royale, so it must be lucky, right? But think about it like a scientist. How many games did you play in it? How many did you lose? Would you have won anyway? To find out whether something really works, you need a fair test.

1. Ask a clear question"Does the Spooky Dash Sprite help me win more fights?"
2. Change only one thingWith the Sprite, or without it. Nothing else.
3. Keep everything else the sameSame mode, same squad, same time of day.
4. Test it lots of timesOne game proves nothing. Twenty is better. A hundred is better still.
5. CompareCount the results with AND without. That second group is called the control.
6. Be honestBelieve the results, even if they're not what you hoped.

Things that go together (but aren't cause and effect)

Here's a famous puzzle. In summer, ice-cream sales go up. In summer, the number of people with sunburn goes up too. Draw a chart, and the two lines rise and fall together. So… does eating ice cream give you sunburn?

Ice cream and sunburn
Made-up numbers, for a city in the Southern Hemisphere

Of course not. Something else causes both of them: hot, sunny weather. When two things rise and fall together, scientists say they're correlated. But correlation doesn't prove that one causes the other. There might be a hidden third thing behind both, or it might just be a coincidence.

The Myth-or-Fact lightning round

Myth: Goldfish forget everything after three seconds

Scientists have trained goldfish to respond to sounds, push levers for food and even find their way through mazes, and the fish remember what they've learned for months. Goldfish are much smarter than the joke says.

Myth: Lightning never strikes the same place twice

Tall, pointy things get struck again and again. The Empire State Building in New York is hit by lightning roughly 20 times a year.

Myth: You can see the Great Wall of China from the Moon

Astronauts say you definitely can't. Even from a space station a few hundred kilometres up, it's very hard to spot with your own eyes, because the wall is long but narrow, and about the same colour as the land around it.

Fact: The first jack-o'-lanterns weren't pumpkins

Fortnitemares has a Snack-O'-Lantern, but the first jack-o'-lanterns were carved from turnips (and sometimes potatoes) in Ireland and Scotland, with a glowing ember inside. Irish folk tales tell of "Stingy Jack", who tricked the Devil and was doomed to wander with only a coal in a hollowed-out turnip to light his way. When people from Ireland and Scotland moved to North America, they found pumpkins were bigger and much easier to carve.

Fact: Fog is a cloud on the ground

The spooky fog rolling across the Island during Fortnitemares is made of the same stuff as a cloud: billions of tiny water droplets. Fog forms when damp air near the ground cools down, and the invisible water vapour in it turns into droplets, just like your breath turns misty on a cold morning.
