The bees who are brilliant at maths

A honeybee colony stores its honey in wax cells, and every cell is a six-sided hexagon. Bees make wax from special glands on the underside of their bodies, and making it takes a lot of energy: they have to eat plenty of honey to produce it. So every bit of wax is precious. The question is: what's the best shape for a cell, so you use the least wax possible?

Remember chapter 4? Only three regular shapes fit together with no gaps: triangles, squares and hexagons. Circles would leave wasted gaps between them. And of those three shapes, the hexagon needs the shortest walls to hold the same amount of honey.

Why bees build hexagons
Circles leave gaps. Hexagons share every wall.

People noticed this a long time ago. More than 2,000 years ago, the Roman writer Varro wondered why bees chose hexagons. Around 1,700 years ago, the Greek mathematician Pappus of Alexandria wrote that bees must have "a certain geometrical forethought". But proving that the hexagon really is the best possible way to divide up a flat surface into equal spaces was incredibly hard. The American mathematician Thomas Hales finally did it in 1999. The bees had known the answer all along; it took humans about 2,000 years to prove it.

Sydney's spiral bees

Australia has its own native bees, and some of them don't sting at all. One stingless bee from the east coast, including around Sydney, has the scientific name Tetragonula carbonaria. It builds the nursery for its babies (called the brood comb) in a spiral, layer after layer, like a spiral staircase made of wax. Scientists are still studying exactly how the bees manage it.

The dance that gives directions

When a honeybee finds a great patch of flowers, she flies home and tells the others where it is. She can't talk, and it's dark inside the hive. So she dances.

The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch spent years watching bees in glass-sided hives and worked out what the dance means. The bee runs in a straight line across the comb, waggling her body from side to side, then loops round and does it again. The direction of the waggle run tells the other bees which way to fly. Straight up the comb means "fly towards the sun". The angle of the run away from straight up is the angle of the food away from the sun. And the longer the waggle run lasts, the further away the food is. Von Frisch shared the Nobel Prize in 1973 for his discoveries about how animals behave.

The bats who share their dinner

The Vampire Sprite steals health from enemies. Real vampire bats, which live in Central and South America, really do drink blood, usually from animals like cows and horses, and usually without waking them. But here's the surprise: they're also some of the most generous animals scientists have ever studied.

A vampire bat has to feed almost every night. If it misses meals for about two or three nights in a row, it can starve. In 1984, a scientist named Gerald Wilkinson published a famous study of vampire bats. He found that when a bat came home hungry, a roost-mate would often share some of its own meal (by bringing it back up: yes, really). And the bats that had been helped were more likely to help back later. They reciprocate. Scientists are still studying exactly how the bats keep track of who helped whom.
