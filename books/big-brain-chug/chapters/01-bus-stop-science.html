Gravity and parachutes

Every match starts the same way. A blue bus, held up by a balloon (don't ask), flies across the Island, and up to a hundred players leap out of the back. You fall. The ground rushes up. At the last moment your glider pops open and you drift down to land.

Now imagine you and a squad-mate jump at exactly the same moment. Your squad-mate is wearing the heaviest, chunkiest armoured outfit in their locker. You're wearing a banana. Who hits the ground first?

In Fortnite, you land together. In real life, the answer took humans about 2,000 years to get right.

Heavy things fall faster. Don't they?

More than 2,300 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle taught that heavy things fall faster than light things. It seems obvious, doesn't it? Drop a rock and a leaf, and the rock wins easily. For nearly 2,000 years, most people simply believed him.

Then along came Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist with a big beard and a bigger habit of asking awkward questions. He had a hypothesis: if you could get rid of the air, a heavy thing and a light thing would fall at exactly the same speed.

Did Galileo really drop balls off the Leaning Tower of Pisa?

It's the most famous story about him: Galileo climbs the Leaning Tower and drops two balls of different weights, and they land together. But historians aren't sure it ever happened. The story comes from a book about Galileo written by one of his students, many years later, and Galileo never described doing it himself. It's a good reminder to ask: who told this story, and how did they know?

What we do know is that Galileo did something cleverer. Falling things move too fast to time with the clocks of his day, so he slowed everything down by rolling balls down long, gentle ramps. Then he measured how far a ball travelled in each equal chunk of time. He found a beautiful pattern, and published it in a book in 1638:

Galileo's pattern: falling faster and faster
A ball dropped with no air to slow it down (real numbers, rounded)
Every second, the ball falls further than the second before, because gravity keeps speeding it up.

In the first second, the ball falls 1 "step". In the next second, 3 steps. Then 5, then 7: the odd numbers! And if you add them up as you go (1, then 1 + 3 = 4, then 4 + 5 = 9, then 16), you get the square numbers. Nature was doing maths all along.

The hammer and the feather

So if Galileo was right, why does a leaf float down slowly while a rock plummets? Because of air resistance. As something falls, it has to shove air out of the way. A leaf is light and spread out, so the air pushes back hard enough to slow it right down. A rock is heavy and compact, so the air barely bothers it.

To prove it, you'd need somewhere with no air at all. Like… the Moon.

On 2 August 1971, at the end of his last moonwalk, the Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott stood in front of the TV camera holding a geology hammer in one hand and a falcon feather in the other. "One of the reasons we got here today," he said, "was because of a gentleman named Galileo, a long time ago, who made a rather significant discovery about falling objects." Then he let go of both at the same moment. With no air to slow the feather, they fell side by side and hit the Moon's dusty ground together. "How about that!" cheered Mission Control in Houston. Scott finished the sentence for them: "Which proves that Mr Galileo was correct in his findings."

Try It: the paper race. Take two identical sheets of paper. Scrunch one into a tight ball and leave the other flat. They weigh exactly the same, so drop them from the same height at the same time. Which lands first? Now you know it's the air, not the weight, that makes the difference.

Falling, with style: the first parachutes

When you fall for a long time, something interesting happens. You speed up and up, but the faster you go, the harder the air pushes back. Eventually the push of the air balances the pull of gravity, and you stop speeding up. A skydiver falling belly-first tops out at about 200 km/h. Fast, but not fast enough to land safely. That's where parachutes come in: a big canopy catches loads of air and makes the air resistance enormous.

Time-Travel Bus: falling with style
c. 1485Leonardo da Vinci sketches a pyramid-shaped parachute made of cloth and wooden poles.
1638Galileo publishes his discoveries about falling objects.
1783Louis-Sébastien Lenormand jumps from a tower in Montpellier, France, with a parachute. He later names it.
1797André-Jacques Garnerin jumps from a balloon high above Paris: the first real parachute jump.
1799Jeanne-Geneviève Garnerin becomes the first woman to make a parachute jump.
1971A hammer and a feather fall together on the Moon. Galileo was right!

The word parachute was invented by Lenormand in France. It means "protection against a fall". His countryman André-Jacques Garnerin made the first jump from really high up, on 22 October 1797. He rode a hydrogen balloon about a kilometre above Paris, cut himself loose in a basket under a folded parachute, and floated down while a crowd watched. His parachute had no hole in the top, so the trapped air spilled out of the sides and he swung wildly from side to side all the way down. He landed shaken, but safe. Later parachutes added a small vent at the top to let air escape smoothly, and the swinging stopped.
