"Don't follow her," said Kai.

"I'm not following her."

"You're standing at the door of the bus, staring at her, waiting for her to jump. That's literally what following is."

Billy didn't answer. The Battle Bus was rumbling across the south of the Island, and the wind was howling in through the open back door, and twenty seats away, sitting perfectly still among the frozen shop-dummy players, the tall dark shape of Nightantler was staring straight ahead. She hadn't looked at him once since the match began. Not once. But Billy knew she knew he was there, the way you always know when someone is pretending not to see you in the playground.

Below them, Nightmare Neighborhood was sliding into view.

Even from the bus it looked wrong. A blanket of fog lay over it, thick and lumpy and faintly glowing, so that only the tops of things poked out: crooked chimneys, the pointed tips of dead trees, a church spire leaning sideways like a drunk old man. Light leaked up through the fog in sickly purples and greens. And in the middle of it all, rising out of the murk like a ship out of a stormy sea, stood the mansion, with its towers and its broken windows and its single lit room at the very top.

Nightantler stood up. She walked to the door. She stepped out of the bus without looking back, and fell.

Billy counted to three. Then he jumped too.

* * *

This time, when his glider opened, it wasn't the old default one. It was the Override 'Brella, his prize for winning: a big, bright umbrella with a canopy that rippled with shifting purple and pink circuits, like a computer chip turned into fabric. It opened above him with a satisfying fwump, and as he drifted down through the fog with it held high over his head, he felt, despite everything, a tiny bit like a superhero.

"Show-off," said Kai.

"You're just jealous."

"I'm extremely jealous. Land on the left. There's a pumpkin on the porch of the green house."

The fog swallowed him. One moment there was purple sky, and the next there was nothing but grey-green murk, cool and damp against his face, and the muffled sound of his own breathing. Then his socks hit a roof, and he slid down the tiles, and dropped onto a porch with a thump.

There was a Pumpkin Chest right beside the door, grinning at him.

Billy didn't do the 360. He just smashed it. CRUNCH. Out came a shield potion, which he drank on the spot, and a Sprite that looked like a little glowing robot with a single huge eye in the middle of its face. TRICK OR TREAT X-RAY SPRITE, said the card.

"X-Ray," said Kai. "That's a good one. It marks enemies near you every few seconds. It's like seeing through walls."

"Seeing through fog?"

"I reckon."

Billy swapped Pip to his pocket, very gently (Pip was not happy about this, and said so), and equipped the X-Ray Sprite. It hopped onto his shoulders and blinked its one big eye. And a few seconds later, out in the fog, a small red diamond appeared, hanging in the air two streets away. A marker. A player.

Then another. And another.

Three red diamonds, scattered through the murk, all moving. And one of them, the furthest, was moving towards the mansion.

* * *

Nightmare Neighborhood had the most Pumpkin Chests on the whole Island, and Billy smashed every one he could find.

He found one in a front garden full of plastic flamingos that had been painted black. He found one in a garage beside an old car with its windows boarded up. He found one in a children's playground, sitting at the bottom of a slide, grinning up at him as if it had come down the slide for fun and stayed there waiting for someone to play with. With every smash, a little more XP fizzed up into the air, and his card ticked slowly towards the next level, and his pockets filled up: bandages, a med kit, a fistful of Slap Candy Corn, a Bonerattler he didn't want, ammo he did.

And all the time, he kept one eye on the red diamonds.

It was the quietest, creepiest place he had ever been. The fog muffled everything. His footsteps made no sound on the cracked pavements. Jack-o'-lanterns sat on every doorstep, flickering, and the only noises were the drip of water from the gutters and the creak of a swing moving gently on its own in an empty garden. Every so often, far away, something howled. Billy decided not to think about what. Kai had told him, on the bus, that there was a boss who lived in the mansion, a guest from a scary old film, and that nobody sensible went anywhere near it, and Billy had decided, firmly and for ever, that he was going to be sensible.

The fourth pumpkin was at the edge of a graveyard.

He smashed it, and it gave him a long wooden box, which sprang open with a click, and lying inside it on a bed of purple velvet was a rifle. A proper rifle, long and slim, with a polished wooden stock and a scope on top that glinted like a spoon.

"Hunting Rifle!" said Kai. "That's your Loot Hack! Billy, that's yours. Nobody else can get that one in this match. It came here just for you."

Billy lifted it out of the box. It was lighter than he had expected, and it balanced perfectly across his two hands, and when he raised it to his eye and looked through the scope, the whole foggy world leapt towards him, magnified, sharp and clear. He swung it slowly across the graveyard. Tombstones, mossy and crooked. A stone angel with a broken wing. A fresh mound of earth, with a shovel stuck in it, where somebody had been digging for keys.

And standing beside the stone angel, perfectly still, with a black shotgun in her hands and her red eyes burning through the fog, Nightantler.

She was looking straight down his scope at him.

* * *

Neither of them moved.

Billy lowered the rifle, very slowly, until he was looking at her with his own eyes. She was twenty metres away. Close enough for her shotgun. Too close, really, for his rifle. And she knew it. He could see that she knew it from the way she was standing, loose and ready, her weight on her toes, like a goalkeeper facing a penalty.

"Billy," whispered Kai. "Billy, back away. Back away right now."

Billy didn't back away.

"I've got a question," he called across the graves. His voice came out higher than he wanted it to. "About the card."

Nightantler said nothing. But she didn't shoot, either.

"When you started," said Billy, "what did your Mastery line say? Before any of the letters filled in?"

There was a long pause. The fog drifted between them. Somewhere a gate creaked.

"W, gap, N," said the girl at last. Her voice was flat. "T, gap, G, gap, gap, T, H, gap, R."

"Same as mine," said Billy. "Exactly the same as mine. Don't you think that's weird? If it was just about winning, why would it say together? Why would the game bother? It's not like the game ever says please or thank you or together about anything."

"Because the game's broken," she said. "Because someone stuck a cursed cartridge into a giant evil machine and now nothing works properly, and you and me are stuck in it, and I've been stuck in it two hours longer than you have." Her voice cracked. "I've been eliminated nine times, Billy. Do you know what that feels like? It feels like being unravelled. Like a jumper with a loose thread, and someone pulling it."

Billy didn't know what to say.

"So don't stand there," said Nightantler, "and tell me what the words mean."

And she raised the shotgun.
