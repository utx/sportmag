There is a moment, in every match of Fortnite, when you have to decide what kind of player you are.

Most of the time you don't notice it. It's tiny. It's the moment when you see somebody else's fight going on in the distance and you decide whether to stay out of it, or to sneak in and pick off whoever's left. It's the moment when you see a player who doesn't know you're there. It's the moment when a teammate goes down and you're the only one who can reach them.

Billy noticed his moment. He was lying on a roof between two giant rubber ducks, looking down the scope of a Hunting Rifle at a girl from Auckland with a sliver of health and a storm on her heels, and two players closing in on her from either side. And for one long second, the whole Island seemed to hold its breath and wait to see what he would do.

The sensible thing, the thing Billy would have done on any other Sunday morning, was obvious. Wait. Let them fight. Let them knock each other out. Pick off whoever was left. That's not cowardly. That's maths.

It was a proper dilemma. On one side, everything the game had ever taught him. On the other side, a girl he barely knew, who had eliminated him less than an hour ago.

"Billy," said Kai quietly. "What are you going to do?"

Billy breathed out, slowly, the way his swimming coach had taught him to breathe at the end of a lap. He laid the crosshairs of the scope on the skull-helmeted player in black armour, just above his shoulder, a little ahead of where he was moving.

"Technically," said Kai, "this is teaming. In solos. It's against the rules."

"Technically," said Billy, "I'm a Sprite."

He squeezed the trigger.

* * *

The Hunting Rifle didn't rattle, like the Bonerattler, or boom, like the shotgun. It went CRACK, sharp and clean, like a branch snapping in a frosty forest, and it kicked against Billy's shoulder like a pony. Far away across the trees, the player in skull armour jerked, and a big number floated up from his head, a headshot number, gold instead of white.

He didn't go down. He was too well armoured for that. But he spun round, completely confused, looking in every direction except up, and Mira's head snapped round too, and in that moment, she saw them. Both of them. The skull, and the pink cat creeping in from the other side.

She moved.

Billy had already gone. He slid down the stripy pipe of the duck diner, hit the ground running, and sprinted into the trees with Pip whooping on his shoulders. Leaves exploded around his ankles. Ahead of him, he heard the shooting start, the boom of a shotgun, the rattle of an SMG, and then a sound he recognised from the first match: the high, crackling whine of the storm, very close now, eating the forest from the south.

He came out of the trees at the back of the research base just as the pink cat player rounded the corner, shotgun up, aiming straight at the upturned trolley where Mira was crouched.

Billy jumped. He jumped again. Pip squeaked. And Billy dashed, in a streak of violet light, straight through the corner of the research base, through a wall, through a desk, through a startled potted plant, and out the other side, right in front of the pink cat.

The pink cat didn't even have time to look surprised.

Billy's Sweeper Shotgun went BOOM, and BOOM, and the pink cat broke apart into a cloud of blue squares and was gone.

Behind him, there was a flat, decisive bang, and another, and then a third, and the skull-helmeted player came apart too, in blue. Mira had stood up from behind the trolley. Her shotgun was smoking. Her health bar was still a thin sliver. And she was looking at Billy.

Neither of them said anything.

Then the storm rolled over the edge of the research base, and they both ran.

* * *

They ran side by side, through the falling leaves, north, out of the purple, with the storm crackling at their backs. It was the first time Billy had ever run alongside someone on the Island who wasn't trying to get him. It felt completely different. It felt like running home from school with your best friend.

They didn't stop until they reached a little stone bridge over a stream, deep inside the new circle. Mira dropped down behind its wall, breathing hard. Billy dropped down beside her.

"Here," he said, and held out his med kit.

Mira looked at it. Then she looked at him. Her red eyes were very bright.

"Why?" she said. "Why did you do that? You could have just let them get me. You'd be one closer to winning."

Billy thought about it, properly, because it seemed like the kind of question that deserved a proper answer.

"Because I think that's the trap," he said at last. "The game wants you to think it's always just you. You against everybody. And mostly it is. But it says together. It says it on both our cards. I reckon if the game only wanted one of us to get out, it would just say win." He shrugged. "And also, you're the only other person in here who knows what it's like. That's got to count for something."

Mira was quiet for a long time. Then she took the med kit, and used it, and her health bar swelled from a sliver to full green.

"Thanks," she said. It came out a bit croaky, as if it hadn't been used in a while.

She reached into her pocket and took something out to show him. It was a Sprite, small and round and fuzzy, golden-yellow with black stripes, and a pair of tiny, glittering wings that hummed so fast they blurred. It made a sound like a very happy fridge.

"Honey," said Mira. "The new one. From Thursday. It sends bees after people you shoot. I got it out of a pumpkin at the start of this match." She stroked its fuzzy head with one bark-dark finger. "Hasn't been extracted. I've never kept anything I found in here. Not once. I always lose it."

Billy reached into his hoodie pocket and took out the Portable Extractor. The heavy little torch-shaped thing, with its glowing blue window. The one he could only buy once a day.

He held it out.

Mira stared at it. "That's yours."

"It doesn't work on me. I tried." He pushed it into her hand. "Go on. Five seconds. Then she's safe forever."

For a moment Billy thought she wasn't going to take it. Then she did. She held the Honey Sprite up to the glowing window, and the Extractor whirred and hummed and counted down, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, and with a soft whoosh the Honey Sprite turned into golden light and was sucked into it, and golden letters bloomed in the air over the bridge.

ADDED TO COLLECTION

Mira let out a breath. And then, for the very first time, Billy heard her laugh. Not the bitter laugh from the lobby. A real one. Surprised and wobbly and delighted.

"Kai says yes," said Billy. "I mean, Kai says: he's sent you a friend request. When we're back in the lobby, accept it. Then he can put us in a squad. All three of us."

"All three?"

"He's coming in," said Billy. "Normal-style. With a controller. From his house." He paused. "He's going to be a banana."

Mira looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded. Just once.

* * *

It was a strange sort of alliance, and they agreed on the rest of it without really discussing it. They wouldn't fight each other. Not this match, not ever again. When the final circle came, they'd simply see what happened.

What happened was this. The shark from the bush lasted until the last three. Billy got him with the Hunting Rifle from two hundred metres away, his best shot of the whole day. And then there were two.

Billy and Mira stood at opposite edges of the last tiny circle, on a hilltop above the stream, with the storm closing in around them like a purple curtain coming down at the end of a play.

"Go on, then," called Mira. "Take it. You've earned it."

"You take it!"

"I've already got one. It's yours." And before Billy could argue, she turned and walked, quite calmly, into the storm. At the very edge of it, she looked back over her shoulder, and her red eyes glowed, and he could have sworn she was smiling.

"See you in the lobby, Aussie," she said.

She dissolved into purple squares. The banner came down out of the sky in a blaze of orange and gold.

#1 VICTORY ROYALE.

It was Billy's second of the morning. It was, without any question at all, the strangest one he'd ever had.
