Billy didn't raise his rifle.

He thought about it. His hands thought about it for him, actually. They twitched, and tightened, and wanted very much to swing the Hunting Rifle up to his shoulder. But he made them stay where they were. He let the rifle hang down by his side, pointing at the grass, and he stood there in the fog among the tombstones, in his socks and his school hoodie, and looked at the girl with the antlers and the shotgun.

"I'm not going to shoot you," he said.

"Then you're an idiot," said Nightantler.

"Probably." Billy swallowed. "But I keep thinking about it. Together. If the only way out is winning together, then it doesn't matter how many times either of us wins on our own. It'll never be enough. We could do this all day. All week. Forever."

The shotgun didn't move. But it didn't fire, either.

"You don't know that," she said. But her voice had changed. There was something underneath it now, something shaky and small, like a crack in the ice.

"No," said Billy. "But neither do you."

For a long moment, the two of them stood there in the graveyard, and the fog curled around the tombstones, and somewhere far off the swing in the empty garden creaked on its chain.

And then the X-Ray Sprite on Billy's shoulder blinked its single eye, and a red diamond appeared in the air.

It was right behind Nightantler. Ten metres behind her, low down, sliding between the graves. Someone was crawling towards her through the fog on their stomach, and she had no idea. She was looking at Billy. She had been looking at Billy the whole time.

Billy didn't think. There wasn't time to think. He did the only thing there was to do: he snapped the Hunting Rifle up to his shoulder and swung it towards the red diamond.

Which meant swinging it towards her.

He saw her eyes widen. He saw her see the rifle come up. He saw her decide, in the smallest fraction of a second, exactly what that meant.

"No, wait..." he started.

The shotgun went off.

* * *

It didn't hurt.

That was what Billy would remember afterwards, and what he would tell Kai, and what he would think about for a long time: that it didn't hurt at all. His shield went with a crash like a dropped plate, and his health bar emptied in a single gulp, and a big red number floated up in front of his face, 126, and then there was a feeling he had no words for.

The nearest he ever got was this. It was like being a jumper, and someone finding a loose thread, and pulling.

He felt himself coming apart. Not painfully. Neatly. Starting at his fingertips, then his hands, then his arms, he was unravelling into tiny glowing purple squares that lifted off him and floated upwards into the fog like sparks off a bonfire. He watched it happen. He couldn't stop it. He looked down and his socks were going, and his knees, and his pyjama shorts, and Pip, safe in his pocket, was squeaking frantically, and the last thing he saw before the purple swallowed his eyes was Nightantler's face.

She had turned around. She was looking at the player crawling through the graves behind her, the one with the shotgun, the one Billy had been aiming at. And then she was looking back at Billy, at the purple squares where Billy had been. And her red eyes were wide with something that looked very much like horror.

Then: nothing.

Then: a stage, floating in the dark.

* * *

Billy sat on the edge of the lobby platform with his legs dangling over the void, the way Nightantler had sat before, and he didn't say anything for a long time.

His card hung in the corner of his vision. He didn't want to look at it. He looked anyway.

TRICK OR TREAT BILLY SPRITE · Lvl 3
MASTERY: WIN TOGETHER

The X-Ray Sprite was gone, lost, because he hadn't extracted it. So were the med kit and the candy corn and the Hunting Rifle. Only Pip was left, sitting on his knee, extracted and safe forever, patting his hand over and over with one little orange paw.

"She was going to get her," Billy said at last. "The other player. He was right behind her. I was trying to help."

"I know," said Kai. His voice was quiet and very angry, not at Billy. "I saw. I saw the whole thing."

"She thought I was going to shoot her."

"Yeah."

"I wasn't."

"I know, mate."

Billy looked out into the endless purple. It was 6.21 in the morning, according to Kai. Fifty-four minutes until Mum came home. He was Level 3, which was worse than he'd been at the end of the first match. He was trapped inside a video game, sitting on a floating drum in the middle of nothing, wearing socks. And the only other person in the whole world who understood what that was like had just eliminated him, because she couldn't imagine that anybody on this Island would ever do anything except try to win.

He had never felt so despondent in his life.

"Billy," said Kai. "Look up."

* * *

In the dark, far above the stage, something like a cinema screen had appeared. It was huge and slightly see-through, and on it, Billy realised, was the match he had just left. Kai was still spectating it on his own screen. Somehow, the lobby was showing Billy too.

On the screen, Nightantler was running.

She had dealt with the player in the graveyard. Billy saw that straight away, from the little pile of loot glowing between the tombstones. Now she was heading north, out of the fog, at a flat sprint, faster than Billy had ever seen anybody run. She wasn't hiding. She wasn't waiting. She was hunting. She went through the rest of that match like the storm itself: relentless, unstoppable, furious. Players went up in clouds of blue squares, one after another, all across the Island. The number in the corner fell and fell.

Billy watched it all. He couldn't look away.

Twelve minutes later, she won.

The gold banner came down. The trumpets played. The confetti fell. And on the giant screen, in the middle of all that celebration, the tall antlered figure stood completely still, with her shotgun hanging from one hand, staring at the card that had appeared in front of her face.

Billy couldn't read it from where he was. He didn't need to. He knew what it said.

Extraction: locked.

The screen faded. And out of the darkness, slowly, a second platform drifted towards Billy's: a dim stage, with low lanterns and a scatter of pumpkins, and a tall figure sitting in the middle of it with her antlered head in her hands.

It drifted closer, and closer, until the two platforms were nearly touching, a metre apart, no more. Close enough to jump. Close enough to talk without shouting.

For a long time, neither of them said anything.

"It didn't work," said the girl, at last. Her voice was muffled behind her hands. "I won. I won by myself. And it didn't work."

"I know," said Billy.

"I saw him. Afterwards. The one behind me." She lifted her head. Her red eyes were dim. "You weren't aiming at me."

"No."

"You were trying to save me. From him."

"Yes."

"And I..." She stopped. When she spoke again, it was in a much smaller voice. "I'm sorry. I'm really, really sorry, Billy. I've been in here so long. Everyone in here's just trying to get me. I forgot that anyone might be trying not to."

Billy thought about saying it was fine. It wasn't fine. But he found, a little to his surprise, that he wasn't angry any more either. He was just tired, and a long way from home, and so was she.

"What's your name?" he said. "Your real one."

The girl was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Mira."

"Where do you live, Mira?"

"Auckland." She wiped her face with the back of one long, dark, bark-rough hand. "Western Springs. Near the zoo. Mum and Dad are taking my little brother to Rainbow's End today. For his birthday. He's turning six. We're meant to leave at ten." She gave a strange, wet little laugh. "I'm going to miss the log flume."

"You won't," said Billy.

"You don't know that."

"No," Billy agreed. "But I've got an idea. It's a stupid idea. It's the stupidest idea I've ever had."

He held out his hand across the gap between the platforms, palm up.

Mira looked at it for a very long time.

She didn't take it.

But she didn't say no, either. And above both their heads, at the very same moment, two words appeared in yellow: READY. READY.
