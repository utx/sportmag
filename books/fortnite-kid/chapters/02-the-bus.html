The first thing Billy noticed was the wind.

It came from everywhere at once, roaring and tugging, flapping his hoodie against his ribs and filling his ears with a sound like the inside of a seashell turned up to full volume. The second thing he noticed was that he was sitting down. The third thing was the smell: hot engine oil, old seat fabric and, faintly, bizarrely, blueberries.

He opened his eyes.

He was on a bus. A big, battered, bright-blue bus with rows of worn seats and handrails worn shiny by a million hands. Hanging under the roof, a fat blue balloon, bigger than a house, was holding the whole bus up in the sky. And through the open doors at the back, where the wind was howling in, there was nothing but air and, far, far below, an island.

The Island.

"No," said Billy, out loud. "No, no, no. No way."

He was on the Battle Bus.

* * *

Other players lined the seats. There were dozens of them: knights and astronauts and a man dressed as a slice of pizza, a woman with a fishbowl for a helmet, someone in a full-body banana suit. But they didn't move like people. They sat with their hands in their laps and stared straight ahead, perfectly still, and when Billy waved a hand in front of the nearest one (a tall soldier with a skull painted on his mask), the soldier didn't even blink. He just kept facing forwards, with a polite and empty expression, like a shop dummy waiting for the shop to open.

Billy had always wondered what the other players were doing on the bus. Now he knew. Nothing. Absolutely nothing at all.

"Billy!" A voice crackled right inside his ear. "Billy, can you hear me? Say something!"

Billy clapped a hand to the side of his head. His headset was still there. He'd been wearing it when he fell, and somehow, impossibly, it had fallen with him.

"Kai!" he shouted over the wind. "Kai, I'm on the bus!"

"I know you're on the bus! I'm spectating you! You just got up and waved at a skeleton!" Kai's voice went up about an octave. "Billy, where are you? Like, actually? Your controller's not even moving. I've been ringing your phone."

"It's on my desk." Billy looked down at himself. His hands were his hands. His hoodie was his faded blue school hoodie, the one with the hole in the cuff he chewed in maths. His pyjama shorts were his pyjama shorts. He was still in his socks. "Kai, I'm in it. I'm in the game. The lightning hit, and the screen went purple, and I just... fell in."

There was a long silence on the line.

"Okay," said Kai finally, in the careful voice of someone who has decided to believe something ridiculous because the alternative is worse. "Okay. Okay. Right. What can you see?"

"The bus. The seats. Everyone's frozen. There's a, um..." Billy looked up. Floating in the air in front of his eyes, as if it were painted on the inside of an invisible pair of glasses, was a set of shapes he knew better than his own handwriting. A blue bar and a green bar at the bottom left. A little square map at the top right. A number: 100. "Kai, I can see the HUD. My health's a hundred. My shield's empty. There are a hundred players."

"Ninety-nine and you," said Kai. "Billy, listen to me. The bus is going to cross the map. You've got to jump. If you don't jump, it kicks you out at the end anyway, and then you'll land wherever the bus stops, and that's usually the worst place."

Billy edged towards the open door at the back. The wind tore at his hair. Below, the Island spread out like a gigantic map somebody had unrolled across the sea, and for a moment, despite everything, despite the terror and the impossibility and the cold feeling in his stomach, Billy simply stared.

He had seen this view thousands of times. He had never seen it like this.

The panorama was enormous. In the very centre of the Island, a vast, dark machine squatted like a spider on the land, all cables and towers and pulsing lights. Geno's Machine. Above it, a storm was spinning. Not the normal storm that crept in from the edges of the map during a match, but a tall, slow, swirling column of purple cloud, lit from inside by flickers of lightning, rising out of the machine like smoke from a chimney. It looked bigger than it did in the patch-note pictures. It looked, Billy thought uneasily, hungry.

To the north-west, the snowy mountains of Lifty Lodge glittered with ski lifts. To the east, Golden Grove blazed red and orange and gold, as if someone had set an autumn forest on fire without any of the fire. Further round, the grassy hills and loop-the-loops of Green Hill Zone rolled like a green sea. And to the south, swaddled in a sickly purple fog that sat on the land like a blanket, was the place everyone had been talking about since the first of October: Nightmare Neighborhood, with its crooked houses and crooked trees and its one big mansion, where every window glowed the colour of a bruise.

Billy's knees went wobbly. He grabbed a handrail.

"Billy." Kai's voice was steady now, steadier than Billy had ever heard it. "Here's the plan. The Power Hour's on. Pumpkin Chests give Sprites every single time. If you're in the game, you need loot, and if you need loot, you need pumpkins. There are five Pumpkin Chests just north of Stone Sanctum. It's close to the middle, and it's quieter than Nightmare. Go there."

Stone Sanctum. Billy found it: an ancient-looking temple of grey stone with towers at the corners and a maze sprawling behind it, coming up fast beneath the bus.

"Kai," he said. "What happens if I get eliminated?"

Another silence. Longer, this time.

"I don't know," Kai said quietly. "So don't."

Billy swallowed. Then, because some habits are stronger than fear, he turned back towards the front of the bus, where a cheerful blue driver's seat sat behind a big round steering wheel. There was nobody in it. There never was. Nobody had ever seen who drove the Battle Bus.

"Thanks, bus driver," said Billy.

The bus horn honked. Twice. Cheerfully.

Billy's mouth fell open. Before he could think too hard about it, he turned, ran three steps, and jumped.

* * *

Falling, it turned out, was the best and worst thing that had ever happened to him.

The wind hit him like a wall and then held him up like a hand. His stomach tried to climb out through his throat. His hoodie flapped so hard it sounded like applause. He tucked his arms in, the way his character always did, and pointed his head at the ground, and he dived, faster and faster, screaming, half in terror and half in a wild, giddy exhilaration he had never felt in his whole life, not even on the big rollercoaster at Luna Park.

A number in the corner of his vision counted down his height. The temple swelled beneath him. He could see the five pumpkins now, glowing purple in the trees north of the walls like the lights of a tiny village.

And he could see something else.

On top of the tallest temple tower, a figure was standing. Tall and thin and dark, with a pair of great, branching, black antlers sprouting from its head like the bare branches of a dead tree. It was looking up. It was looking straight at him. And its eyes, in the shadow of those antlers, glowed a steady, burning red.

Billy blinked, and in the blink, his glider snapped open above him with a whump that jerked him upright, and the antlered figure stepped backwards off the tower, and was gone.
