You have to choose

Fortnite is a game of choices. Which spot to land at. Which gun to keep. Whether to open that chest or run from the storm. Whether to trust the player waving at you from the top of a hill. (Don't.) Most of those choices last a second and are forgotten a second later.

This book is different. Every page asks you a question that's impossible to answer, and then refuses to let you off the hook. Would you rather have one Chug Jug or six Small Shield Potions? Live in a maze or a temple whose walls keep vanishing? Have Wonkee as your maths teacher? Win one match now, or three next month?

Each one is a dilemma: a choice where both options have something good about them and something bad about them, so that whichever you pick, you lose something. That's what makes them fun. And, sneakily, it's what makes them hard.

The rules

  1. You must choose. A or B. You can't skip, and you can't say "both". If you say "neither", the storm gets you.
  2. You must say why. A choice without a reason is just a coin toss. Every answer starts with "I'd choose… because…".
  3. "It depends" is allowed, but only once you've said what it depends on. "It depends whether I'm playing solo or in a squad" is a brilliant answer. "It depends" on its own is a way of hiding.
  4. You're allowed to change your mind. If someone gives a better reason than yours, switching sides isn't losing. It's thinking.

Most of these questions are hypothetical. Nobody is really going to make you dance every time you open a chest (see Chapter 5). But plenty of them are about the real game, as it was on the Island in October 2026, and those use real facts about real items, places and powers. Where a choice is about the actual game, we've checked the details. Where it's pure imagination, we'll say so.

Warm-up

The case for A: Jumping first means you land first, and the first player on the ground grabs the first weapon. You also get the pick of every place the bus passes in the opening seconds.

The case for B: Jumping last means you can watch where everyone else goes, and choose somewhere they're not. And because the bus flies a different route every match, the last stop will take you somewhere new every time.

The catch: First means you might land next to fifteen other players who also jumped first. Last means you could land at the very edge of the Island and spend the whole match outrunning the storm.

Did you decide? Good. Now try to justify it in one sentence, starting with "because". That's the whole skill of this book, and you've just done it once.

How to win an argument (fairly)

The best would-you-rather debates aren't won by the loudest person. They're won by the person with the best rationale. Here's a three-step recipe that works on everything from Chug Jugs to bedtimes:

The three-step argument
1
MAKE YOUR POINT
"I'd rather jump last."
2
GIVE EVIDENCE
"Last time I jumped first, three players landed on the same roof."
3
EXPLAIN IT
"So jumping first doesn't guarantee a weapon. Jumping last guarantees some peace."

Point, evidence, explanation. Most people stop after step one ("I just would, OK?"). Some manage step two. Very few bother with step three, which is the one that actually changes people's minds.

Off the Island: the donkey who couldn't decide

Philosophers have a famous puzzle about a hungry donkey. It stands exactly halfway between two haystacks. The haystacks are exactly the same size and smell exactly as delicious. The donkey has no reason to prefer one over the other, so it can't decide which way to walk, and (the story goes) it stands there until it starves.

The puzzle is called Buridan's ass ("ass" being an old word for donkey), after Jean Buridan, a French philosopher who lived in the 1300s. Funnily enough, the donkey doesn't actually appear in any of Buridan's own writings that survive. His critics seem to have invented it to make fun of his ideas about choice. Much older versions of the puzzle exist too: the ancient Greek thinker Aristotle wrote about a man who was exactly as hungry as he was thirsty, standing between food and drink.

The lesson? When two choices are truly equal, the worst thing you can do is nothing. Pick one. The donkey would have been perfectly happy with either haystack. In Fortnite, a player who hovers over the map for twenty seconds deciding between two equally good drops ends up landing in the sea.

How to use this book

  • On your own: Decide before you read the arguments. Then read both sides and see if anything changes your mind. At the back there's a quiz that tells you what kind of chooser you are.
  • With friends or family: Read the question aloud. Everyone votes. Then each side gets thirty seconds to persuade the other. Vote again. Did anyone switch?
  • On a long car trip: It turns out there are few better ways to survive one.
  • The Chug Jug verdict boxes are our own opinion. You're allowed to disagree. In fact, we'd be a little disappointed if you never did.
  • Word Watch boxes explain the most useful words, and the Word Locker at the back collects them all.
