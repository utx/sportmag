You've made thirty-five impossible choices. You've argued, switched sides and possibly fallen out with a sibling over a banana. Now it's time to look behind the curtain: how do choices and arguments actually work, and how do you make a would-you-rather so good that nobody can answer it?

Spot the sneaky argument

Here's a secret about this whole book: every would-you-rather is a trick. In real life, you're almost never stuck with only two options. You could have one Chug Jug and some Small Shields. You could land at WonkeeLand some of the time. The game works because we deliberately pretend there are only two choices.

That's fine in a game. But in a real argument, pretending there are only two options is a sneaky move, and it has a name: a false dilemma. It belongs to a family of tricks called fallacies: arguments that sound convincing but don't actually hold up. Here are three of the most common. Once you know them, you'll hear them everywhere: in adverts, on the news, and in every argument about whose turn it is.

Three sneaky arguments
1
FALSE DILEMMA
"Either we land at WonkeeLand, or you're scared." (There are dozens of other options.)
2
BANDWAGON
"Everyone uses the Spooky Dash, so it must be the best." (Popular doesn't mean right.)
3
STRAW MAN
"You want a quiet drop? So you want to hide all game and never fight?" (That's not what they said.)

The name "straw man" comes from the idea of building a scarecrow version of your opponent, made of straw, and knocking that over instead of the real thing. It's much easier to beat an argument nobody actually made.

Decide like a scientist

When a choice is genuinely hard, there's a tool that helps: a decision table. It turns a messy feeling into numbers you can compare. Here's how it works, using dilemma #8: WonkeeLand every match, or the quiet island every match.

Step 1. List what matters to you. Let's say: loot, safety and fun.

Step 2. Decide how much each one matters, from 1 to 3. This is called its weight. Say loot matters a lot (3), safety matters a bit (2), and fun matters a little (1).

Step 3. Score each option out of 5 on each thing.

Step 4. Multiply each score by its weight, and add them up.

What mattersWeightWonkeeLandQuiet island
Loot34 × 3 = 122 × 3 = 6
Safety21 × 2 = 25 × 2 = 10
Fun15 × 1 = 52 × 1 = 2
Total1918

WonkeeLand wins, just. But watch what happens if safety matters a lot to you, and you give it a weight of 3 instead of 2. WonkeeLand's safety score becomes 1 × 3 = 3, and its total drops to 20. The quiet island's becomes 5 × 3 = 15, and its total jumps to 23. Now the island wins comfortably.

That's the most important lesson of the whole book, hiding in a table. The same facts can lead to different answers, depending on what you care about. When you and a friend disagree about a would-you-rather, it's often not because one of you is wrong. It's because you've weighted things differently. Working out what you each care about is how arguments turn into conversations.

Try it yourself: make a decision table for dilemma #2 (one Chug Jug or six Small Shields). Pick three things that matter, give them weights, and see which wins. Then change one weight and see if the answer flips.

Build your own would-you-rather

Making a great would-you-rather is harder than it looks. Here's a terrible one:

"Would you rather have a Legendary weapon or a grey one?"

Nobody has to think about that. Everybody picks the Legendary. A good would-you-rather needs four ingredients:

  1. Balance. Both options must be equally tempting (or equally awful). If everyone picks the same answer, it isn't a dilemma.
  2. Detail. "Be a llama" is OK. "Be a loot llama, so everyone wants to smash you, but you're full of treasure" is much better. Details give people something to argue about.
  3. A catch. The best options look great until you spot the hidden downside. Flying sounds perfect until you realise everybody can see you.
  4. Something real underneath. The greatest would-you-rathers are secretly about something bigger: safety versus excitement, now versus later, being liked versus being right.

Here's the terrible one, fixed: "Would you rather have a Legendary weapon that plays a loud kazoo noise every time you fire it, or a grey one that's completely silent?" Now it's about power versus stealth, with a big dose of silliness. Much better.

Fill in the gaps to make your own:

  • Would you rather be able to ________ but ________, or ________ but ________?
  • Would you rather land at ________ every match, or never be allowed to land there again?
  • Would you rather have a Sprite that ________, or one that ________?
  • Would you rather your squad always ________, or never ________?
  • Would you rather live inside ________ for a week, or ________ for a day?

Test your creations on your family. If they groan "Oh, that's hard!", you've made a good one.
