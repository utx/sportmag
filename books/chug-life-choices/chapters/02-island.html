Chapter 2 · Places
Island hopping

The Island is enormous, ridiculous and constantly changing. It has a haunted theme park, a temple with disappearing walls, a city with a rubber-duck race, a hedge maze with a teleporting toilet, and a ski lodge with a cable car. All on the same map. In real life, you'd need a passport, a lot of aeroplanes and a very understanding family to visit them all.

So: where would you go? And, more importantly, where would you stay?

Chaos or calm?

The facts: WonkeeLand is a haunted theme park in the north-east, with 34 chests, a roller-coaster you can grind along, and a reputation as "the chaos drop" of the season. The quiet east island, between WonkeeLand and Golden Grove, has no name on the map at all. It has a dock, a boat with a chest in it, some fishing spots, two ziplines back to the mainland and, according to one guide, it "barely gets any traffic".

The case for A: Every match starts with a bang. You'd get brilliant at fighting, fast, because you'd have to. Within a month you'd be one of the most fearless players on the Island.

The case for B: Every match starts in peace. You'd always reach the middle of the game fully kitted out, and you'd get loads of practice at the part of the match that actually decides who wins: the end.

The catch: A would be exhausting. B might get monotonous: the same dock, the same boat, the same fish, every single time. Which would wear you down first: the chaos or the boredom?

Home sweet home

The facts: Stone Sanctum is an ancient temple where some walls and platforms appear and vanish depending on how close you are to them. Under it is a secret vault, opened by draining a pool. Mega Maze is a giant hedge maze with trap buttons hidden on its paths, a vault key that wanders around on its own, and an emergency exit in the form of a portable toilet that zaps you out to the maze's south entrance.

The case for A: It's beautiful, it's mysterious, and there's a hidden treasure room under the living room. Your friends would never stop asking to visit. The vanishing walls are just a quirk. You'd get used to them.

The case for B: Privacy! Nobody could ever find your bedroom. Unwanted visitors would get lost, step on a trap and leave. And the toilet doubles as a front door, which saves on plumbing.

The catch: Imagine walking to the kitchen in Stone Sanctum at night and finding that the stairs aren't there any more. Now imagine trying to find the kitchen at all in Mega Maze. Both houses are, to put it politely, inhospitable.

The last circle

The facts: Heatwave Harbor is a sunny, Miami-style city built on two islands joined by a bridge, with canals running between the buildings. Lifty Lodge is a ski lodge with a cable car, high in the snowy north-west, with one of the biggest chest counts on the map.

The case for A: Water everywhere. You could swim between hiding spots, and the canals make it easy to get around. Plus, if you're bored while waiting for the circle, there's a Duck Race.

The case for B: High ground. Snowy slopes. A cable car to ride up the mountain. And all those chests and vending machines mean you could arrive fully stocked.

The twist, and it's a big one: In this hypothetical, the circle always ends in the same place. Think about what that means. If you know it, so does everyone else. Every single player would rush there at the start, and the "final circle" would turn into a hundred-player brawl in the first minute. The rule would change how the whole game is played.

That's a lesson that goes way beyond Fortnite. When a secret stops being a secret, it stops being an advantage. Sports coaches and game designers see it all the time: once everyone copies the same winning trick, it stops being a winning trick.

Lost senses

The case for A: You'd still hear everything: footsteps, chests humming, gunfire in the distance, the storm rumbling closer. Sound tells you where danger is. You'd learn the Island by heart, the way you know your own street.

The case for B: The map tells you where the storm is, where you are, and where you need to go. Without it, you could easily wander the wrong way and get swallowed by the storm. And Fortnite has a setting called Visualise Sound Effects that shows sounds as little icons on screen, so you wouldn't be completely deaf.

Off the Island: animals with no maps

Plenty of animals navigate brilliantly without maps. Bats hunt in total darkness by shouting very high-pitched squeaks and listening for the echoes, which tell them where every moth and branch is. It's called echolocation, and some blind people have learned to do a version of it by clicking their tongues.

Sea turtles are even more astonishing. A young loggerhead turtle can swim thousands of kilometres around an ocean and, years later, find its way back to the coast where it hatched. Scientists believe one of the tricks they use is sensing the Earth's magnetic field: a built-in compass. So if you chose "no map", you're in good company. You'd just need to become part bat, part turtle.

Boat or car?

The facts: The Motorboat seats four, is very tough, and has a missile launcher on the front. It can even drive on land, but it slowly takes damage while it does. The Whiplash is a sleek sports car: very fast on roads, not so good off them, it only fits two, and it needs fuel.

The case for A: The Island is criss-crossed with rivers, lakes and canals, and surrounded by sea. A boat goes nearly everywhere, the whole squad fits in it, and it has a missile launcher. A missile launcher.

The case for B: Speed, style and roads that go almost everywhere a boat doesn't. When the storm is closing in, nothing outruns a sports car on a good road.

The catch: A boat on land is slowly falling apart. A car with no fuel is a very expensive chair.

Off the Island: the most remote homes

Here's a real-life version of dilemma #9, for anyone who thinks a maze sounds lonely. Would you rather live on the International Space Station or at an Antarctic research station?

The Space Station flies about 400 kilometres above the Earth, going all the way around roughly every 90 minutes. That means the astronauts on board see about sixteen sunrises and sixteen sunsets every day. People have been living on it non-stop since November 2000. You'd float everywhere, sleep in a sleeping bag strapped to a wall, and drink water that was recycled from… well, let's just say it's been recycled very thoroughly.

Australia runs three research stations on the Antarctic mainland: Mawson, Davis and Casey. Mawson, opened in 1954, is the oldest station south of the Antarctic Circle that has been open ever since. In winter, the Sun hardly rises, ferocious winds howl off the ice, and supply ships can't get through. The scientists who stay there are some of the most remote people on the planet.

Both are formidable places to live. Both have amazing views. Only one of them has penguins.
