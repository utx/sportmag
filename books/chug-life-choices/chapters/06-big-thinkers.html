Chapter 6 · Deep questions
Big thinkers

These are the hardest questions in the book. Not because the options are difficult to imagine, but because your answer says something about what you value. Grown-ups argue about questions like these, and philosophers have written whole books about them.

Take your time. There's no storm in this chapter.

Now or later?

The case for A: A win today is certain. A month is a long time, and who knows what might happen? The season might end. The game might change. You might lose interest. A bird in the hand, as the saying goes, is worth two in the bush.

The case for B: Three is more than one. Three times more, in fact. A month isn't really that long, and patience pays.

Which you choose says a lot about how you think about time. Wanting the reward right now is called seeking instant gratification, and everybody feels the pull of it. It's why it's so hard to save pocket money, and so easy to eat a biscuit before dinner.

Off the Island: the marshmallow test

In the 1960s and 1970s, a psychologist named Walter Mischel ran a famous experiment at Stanford University in America. A young child sat at a table with one marshmallow (or another treat) in front of them. The researcher explained: "You can eat this one now. Or, if you wait until I come back, you can have two." Then the researcher left the room, sometimes for as long as fifteen minutes.

Some children gobbled the marshmallow straight away. Some covered their eyes, sang to themselves, or turned their chairs around so they couldn't see it. Some managed to wait the whole time.

Years later, the researchers found that the children who had waited tended to do better at school as teenagers. For a while, people took this to mean that self-control was a sort of magic key to success. But here's where it gets interesting. Later scientists repeated the experiment with many more children from many different backgrounds, and the link turned out to be much weaker than first thought. Other studies found that children waited much longer if they trusted the grown-up to keep their promise. If the researcher had broken an earlier promise, the children reasonably ate the marshmallow straight away!

So the lesson isn't simply "waiting is good". It's more like this: waiting makes sense when you trust the reward will really come. That's exactly the question in dilemma #30.

The perfect secret

The case for A: You'd never be caught in the storm. You could find the perfect hiding spot in the final circle before anyone else even knows where it is, and wait there with the high ground while everyone else fights their way in.

The case for B: You'd have the best loot in the lobby every single match. No wasted time searching empty houses, just straight from chest to chest, fully kitted out in two minutes.

The catch: Knowing the end doesn't help if you don't survive the beginning. Having the best loot doesn't help if the storm catches you. Each secret covers half the match. Which half do you find harder? Your answer is a clue about which part of your game needs the most practice.

(Interesting fact: one Sprite in the game, the Storm Scout, really does show you where future storm circles will be, but only once you've levelled it all the way up. It's one of the most useful powers in Fortnite, and one of the slowest to unlock.)

Famous or secret?

The case for A: One perfect moment, seen by millions. You'd be remembered. People would talk about it for years.

The case for B: A hundred wins! You'd be one of the best players who ever lived. You'd just be anonymous.

The deeper question: Do you do things because you enjoy them, or because other people see you doing them? Most of us would honestly say "a bit of both". But it's worth thinking about which matters more. People who do things mainly for the enjoyment tend to keep going for longer, because they don't need anyone's applause to carry on. And fame can be a strange thing. Some people become famous for good reasons. Others become notorious: well known for something bad. Fame on its own doesn't tell you which kind.

Win wrong, or lose right?

The case for A: A win is a win. It wasn't your fault the game glitched. Nobody would ever know.

The case for B: You'd know. Losing fairly after playing brilliantly means you can be proud of every decision you made. And you'd have learned something for next time.

This is a question about integrity: doing the right thing even when nobody is watching. Most people, when they're honest, would feel a little hollow about the glitch win. It's like getting full marks because the teacher accidentally marked the wrong paper. The mark looks nice, but it doesn't mean anything.

(And for the record: deliberately using glitches, hacks or cheats is against Fortnite's rules and can get an account banned. This book will never tell you how. The only shortcuts in here are the official ones.)

Time travel

The case for A: Fortnite Battle Royale launched in September 2017, and some players still feel nostalgic for the original Island, with places like Pleasant Park, Retail Row and Loot Lake. You'd see where it all began. No Sprites, no Overrides, no Zero Build (that didn't arrive until 2022). Just a bus, a map, and building. Lots of building.

The case for B: Imagine what Fortnite might look like in ten years. New islands, new ideas, maybe technology that doesn't even exist yet. You'd be the first person from today to see it.

The catch: The past is safe: you know it was good, because people still talk about it. The future is a gamble: it could be amazing, or it could be completely unrecognisable. And here's a strange thought: the version of Fortnite you're playing right now, with its Sprites, Overrides and Duck Race, will one day be "the old days" that players feel nostalgic about. Enjoy it while it's here.

The last question

The case for A: Never losing! Every match a win. Who needs to improve when you're already unbeatable?

The case for B: Getting better is the fun part. The first time you win a fight you'd have lost a month ago is one of the best feelings in any game. And the losing doesn't last. Getting better does.

The catch with A: Winning would very quickly become monotonous. If you can't lose, the wins don't mean anything. It's like playing a game against someone who lets you win every time. Nice for a day. Boring for a week.

A psychologist called Carol Dweck spent years studying how people think about learning. She found that some people believe their abilities are fixed: you're either good at something or you're not. Others believe abilities grow with effort and practice, so a mistake is just information about what to try next. She called the second way of thinking a growth mindset, and found that it often helps people keep going when things get hard.

Option B is a growth mindset in would-you-rather form. Losing a lot and improving every time is how every great player in history got great. It's also how people get good at maths, music, football, reading and pretty much everything else. Nobody is born brilliant at Fortnite. They just lost more matches than you've played yet, and learned something from each one. That's what resilient players do.

The Chug Jug verdict

B. Every time. (And if you chose A, we suspect you're going to change your mind in about a week.)
