Chapter 4 · Teammates
Squad goals

Playing solo, every decision is yours. Playing in a squad, every decision belongs to four people, and four people almost never agree on anything. Where to land. Who gets the shotgun. Whether to revive the player who just ran into the storm for no reason (again).

The questions in this chapter aren't really about Fortnite. They're about people, and they're some of the most useful questions in the book.

Skill or kindness?

The case for A: They're brilliant. They land the shots, win the fights and carry the squad to Victory Royales. Who cares if they're a bit full of themselves?

The case for B: An affable teammate makes every match enjoyable, win or lose. They call out enemies calmly, they say "nice try" when you miss, and they make the whole squad play better because nobody's afraid to make a mistake.

The catch: The arrogant player never listens. When they're wrong (and everyone is wrong sometimes), nobody can tell them. They charge into fights alone, sure they can win, and drag the squad down with them. Meanwhile, the friendly average player gets a little better every week, because people actually want to play with them.

The Chug Jug verdict

B, and it isn't close. Skill can be learned. Being pleasant to play with is rarer, and it lasts. Coaches in real sports say the same thing: a team of good players who trust each other often beats a team of stars who don't.

Who decides?

The case for A: It's fair. Everyone has a say, and nobody lands somewhere they hate. When a decision is unanimous, everyone is committed to it.

The case for B: It's fast. The Battle Bus doesn't wait for anyone, and a squad that argues for thirty seconds ends up jumping into the sea. One leader, one marker on the map, go.

The catch: Four people agreeing on anything within thirty seconds is extremely unlikely. You'd almost certainly end up with a compromise: somewhere nobody chose and nobody loves, but that nobody hates either. Meanwhile, under rule B, the leader might pick WonkeeLand every single match, forever.

A smart middle way: Take turns. One player picks each match. Everyone gets a go, the decision is still fast, and nobody can complain for long, because their turn is coming. It's how plenty of families decide which film to watch, and it works.

Off the Island: the ox and the crowd

In 1906, a scientist called Francis Galton (a cousin of Charles Darwin) went to a country fair in Plymouth, in England. There was a competition: guess the weight of a large ox, once it had been butchered. About eight hundred people bought tickets and wrote down their guesses. Some were farmers and butchers who knew a lot about cattle. Most weren't.

Galton expected the crowd to be hopeless. Instead, when he looked at all the guesses together, he found something astonishing. The middle guess was 1,207 pounds. The ox's real weight was 1,198 pounds. The crowd, taken as a whole, was out by less than 1 per cent. And when the guesses were averaged, the answer came within about a pound. Many individuals were wildly wrong, but their mistakes cancelled each other out.

This idea is now called the wisdom of crowds. But notice how it worked: everyone guessed on their own, without copying each other or arguing. If they'd all listened to the loudest person first, the magic would have disappeared. So the best way to decide as a squad might be this: everyone says their choice at the same moment, before anyone can persuade anyone. Then go with the most popular.

Sharing is caring?

The case for A: You win and lose together. A teammate with a great weapon makes the whole squad stronger, and next time, they might return the favour. Being loyal builds trust.

The case for B: You found it. And if you're the best player in the squad, the best weapon is most useful in your hands. Giving it away just to be polite could lose the match.

The clever answer: Neither "always" is quite right. The best squads give each weapon to the player who'll use it best. If your friend is brilliant with a sniper rifle and you're hopeless, the sniper goes to them, even if you found it. If you're the shotgun expert, the shotgun comes to you. Economists have a similar idea about countries and jobs: everyone does best when each person concentrates on what they're best at, and they share. In Fortnite, it means a squad where everyone's carrying the right tool.

Talk or silence?

The case for A: You'd always know what they're doing. Every enemy they see, every chest they open, every thought they have about lunch. Somewhere in all that talk, there's useful information.

The case for B: Peace. You can concentrate. And a quiet teammate never accidentally gives away your position, or distracts you in the final circle with a story about their cat.

The real lesson: The best callouts are concise. "Two players, behind the blue house, one's low" tells your squad everything they need in five seconds. A verbose teammate buries that same information under a minute of chatter, and by the time they get to the point, it's too late. Good writing works the same way: say what matters, clearly, and stop.

Credit where it's due

The case for A: You'd know the truth. You'd have the satisfaction of a match well played, and your squad would have won. Isn't that what matters?

The case for B: Fame! Glory! Admiration! Everyone thinks you're a legend, and you didn't even have to try.

The catch: Option B sounds great until the next match, when everyone expects you to do it again. And you can't. Being admired for something you didn't do is a bit like wearing a costume: it looks impressive, but you're always worried someone will find out.

Most people, when they really think about it, choose A. Being good matters more to us than looking good. But it's worth noticing how much we all want to be noticed, too. That's not a bad thing. It just means that when someone on your squad does something brilliant, you should tell them. Everyone deserves to have their good work seen.

Lightning round

  • A squad of your three best friends who are all terrible at Fortnite, or three strangers who are all amazing?
  • Always be the one who gets revived, or always be the one who does the reviving?
  • Play every match with your teacher, or with your grandparent?
  • A teammate who's always cheerful but always lost, or always grumpy but always knows the way?
  • Win as a squad but get no eliminations yourself, or get ten eliminations but your squad finishes 20th?
