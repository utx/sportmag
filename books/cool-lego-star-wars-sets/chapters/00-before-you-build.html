Before you build

Right now, in November 2026, there are 74 LEGO Star Wars sets on sale. The smallest is a 58-piece speeder bike. The biggest is a 9,023-piece Death Star. Some are brilliant. A few are, to put it politely, underwhelming. This book reviews every single one, so you know which is which.

Nobody could build 74 sets in a week (well, nobody with homework), so we did what good reporters do: we read the experts. For most sets, that means the detailed reviews on Brickset, one of the biggest LEGO fan websites in the world, where reviewers build each set, photograph every stage and give an honest verdict. We also looked at what thousands of Brickset members scored each set. When we say "the reviewer found", we mean Brickset's reviewer, not us.

How the ratings work

Every set gets a score out of five bricks. That's our overall opinion. Under it are three smaller scores, also out of five:

Play: how much fun it is to actually play with. Does it swoosh? Does it have hatches, launchers and minifigures to make stories with?

Display: how good it looks on a shelf. Is it accurate to the film or show? Would a visitor say "whoa"?

Value: how much you get for the money: pieces, minifigures, size and fun, compared with its price.

Those three things don't always agree. A giant display model might score 5 for Display and 1 for Play. A cheap Battle Pack might be the other way round. Thinking about different criteria separately is what real critics do, and it's why a set can be "great" and "not worth it" at the same time.

Every set also has a From the screen box: the story behind it. Which film or show it comes from, what happens in that scene, and the odd bit of behind-the-scenes trivia. Watch out for the story words in those boxes. They're some of the best words in the book.

For each set you also get Wins (the good stuff) and Watch outs (the problems), the official photo, extra photos from Brickset's hands-on reviews and, where there are any, a line-up of the minifigures in the box.

A final decision or opinion after looking at all the evidence. Judges give verdicts in court; reviewers give verdicts on LEGO sets.
Criterianoun (plural)
The things you use to judge something. One criterion, two criteria.
Underwhelmingadjective
Less impressive than you hoped. The opposite of overwhelming.
Currencynoun
The kind of money a country uses, like Australian dollars, US dollars or British pounds.
Exchange ratenoun
How much of one currency you get for another. If one Australian dollar buys less than one US dollar, prices in Aussie dollars look bigger.
Retireverb
For a LEGO set: to stop being made and sold. Retired sets often become hard to find and expensive.

A word about prices

The prices in this book are LEGO's official prices in the United States, in US dollars (written US$). We use them because they're the same everywhere online and easy to compare. Australian prices are different, and the numbers are usually bigger, because of the exchange rate: one Australian dollar is worth less than one US dollar, so you need more of them. Shops also have sales, so the real price can be lower. For the Aussie price, check the LEGO shop or a toy shop with a grown-up.

The "Per piece" number is the price divided by the number of pieces, in US cents. It's a rough way to measure value. Lower is better, but it isn't everything: a big printed canopy or a minifigure is worth a lot more than a tiny round plate.

Ask a grown-up. This book is for reading, dreaming and making wish lists, not for nagging. Lots of these sets cost a lot of money, and the best set is often the one you already own and haven't built in a new way yet.

Leaving soon?

LEGO sets don't stay in shops forever. After a year or two, most retire. Brickset keeps a list of when it expects each set to leave, and several in this book are due to go at the end of 2026. You'll see a Leaving soon? note on those. It's an expectation, not a promise, so don't panic.

How old do you need to be?

The age on the box (like 9+ or 18+) is a guide to how tricky the build is and who it was designed for, not a law. Many "18+" sets are simply display models aimed at adult collectors: lots of repetition, fiddly details and not much to play with. A patient nine-year-old who has built a few big sets can manage most of them, with help. The big question is usually whether you'd enjoy it, not whether you could do it.

One more thing

This is an unofficial fan book. It isn't made, approved or checked by LEGO or by Lucasfilm (the company that makes Star Wars). The opinions are ours, and in places they're grumpy. When something is expensive, we say so. When something is brilliant, we say that too.
