Chapter 9
Worth its weight in Chug?

By now you can do something most players can't: look at a treasure and work out what it really costs.

Not in V-Bucks. Treasures can't be bought. They cost something more precious, something you can never earn back: time.

What does 688 hours look like?

The average wait for Pirate Shop Access, hunting flat-out, was 688 hours. That's just a number until you compare it with something.

  • A school day is about six hours long. 688 hours is about 115 school days: more than half a school year.
  • If you played for one hour every single day, 688 hours would take nearly two years. The season lasts five weeks.
  • A football match lasts 90 minutes. You could watch 458 matches, which is more than twelve entire Premier League seasons of your favourite club.
  • People who study learning say it takes somewhere around 600 to 750 hours of study for an English speaker to get good at a language like Spanish or French.

And remember, 688 hours is only the average. More than a third of hunters would spend all that time and still have nothing.

Economists (people who study how we make choices) have a name for this way of thinking: opportunity cost. The real cost of anything is whatever you gave up to get it. An hour spent opening chests is an hour not spent playing a match properly with your friends, kicking a ball, reading, or doing nothing at all, which is underrated.

Why rare things feel so valuable

Here's a strange fact about human brains: we want things more when they're hard to get. Shops know this ("Only 3 left!"). So do game designers. It's called scarcity.

Treasure Hunters stacks up three kinds of scarcity at once. The treasures are rare (long odds). They're exclusive (you can't buy them). And the weekly ones are on a timer: when the week's guest star changes, that week's Gold Outfit is replaced by the next one.

The timer is the powerful one. It creates FOMO, the fear of missing out. If a prize will always be there, you can relax. If it vanishes on Sunday, a little voice says hurry.

There's nothing wicked about this. Limited-time things are fun, and they're part of what makes a season feel like an event. But it's worth knowing when that voice is talking, because FOMO is a feeling, not a fact. Ask it a question back: "If I never got this, would I still care in a month?"

The sunk cost trap

The gambler's fallacy has a cousin, and it catches hunters who've already put in a lot of hours. It sounds like this: "I've opened 20,000 chests. I can't stop now, or all that time was wasted."

That's the sunk cost fallacy. The 20,000 chests are behind you. They're "sunk", like a ship on the seabed, and nothing you do next will raise them. The only question that matters is about the future: is the next hour of hunting worth more to you than the next hour of anything else?

Chests have no memory, remember. They don't know you've been loyal. The 20,001st chest gives you exactly the same odds as the first.

The smart pirate's rules

1. Play the game you'd play anyway. Every chest you open during a normal, fun match is a free ticket in the lottery. That's the best way to hunt: by accident.

2. Open chests in a squad. Four friends open chests about four times as fast as one, and by all accounts a find is shared with the whole squad. It's also just more fun.

3. Pick your moments. If you want the Vehicle, a Treasure Hour makes a real difference. For Gizmos and Jam Tracks, any time is as good as any other.

4. Decide your limit before you start. "I'll play until dinner" is a plan. "I'll play until I get it" is a trap, because "it" may never come.

5. Never chase. If you catch yourself thinking "I'm due" or "I can't stop now", close this book, put down the controller and go outside. You've just spotted two fallacies in your own head, which is something to be proud of.

What about the people who did find the token?

Good for them! Somebody has to. With millions of players opening millions of chests, there will be winners every day. They didn't hunt better or want it more. They flipped seventeen heads in a row, and they'd be the first to tell you so.

The right way to feel about somebody else's luck is the way you'd feel if a friend found a ten-dollar note on the footpath: pleased for them, and no poorer yourself.
