When the odds came out, the first thing everybody did was turn them into time. If you open 200 chests an hour, how long until a treasure turns up?

Those numbers flew around the internet within hours. And lots of people described them like this: "If you play for 688 hours, you're guaranteed the token." Or: "688 hours is the worst luck possible."

Both of those are wrong. You already know enough to see why.

An average is not a promise

The numbers in that table are averages. If a huge crowd of players all hunted at 200 chests an hour, and you added up all their waiting times and shared them out equally, each player's share would be 688 hours.

But nobody gets the average. Some find the token in their very first chest. (It has happened: one was reported on the first day of the season.) Some find it after 100 hours. And some go far, far beyond 688.

How far? Use the at-least-one trick. 688 hours at 200 an hour is 137,500 chests: exactly as many chests as the odds. From Chapter 3, that means a 63% chance of having found it. So after 688 hours of non-stop hunting, 37 out of every 100 players still have nothing.

There is no "worst luck possible". There's no number of chests after which the game must pay up. You can always, always miss again.

Mean, median and the unlucky few

Look at the second bar of that chart. Half of the hunters have the token after 477 hours, well before the 688-hour average. That halfway point is called the median.

Why is the average so much higher than the median? Because of the unlucky few at the far end. A hunter who takes 3,000 hours drags the average up a long way, just as one very tall basketball player would raise the average height of your class without making anybody else taller.

So here's a fairer way to describe the token: half of all non-stop hunters would find it within about 477 hours (that's nearly 20 days without sleep). The other half would take longer. Some would take much longer.

Chests have no memory

Now for the most important idea in the whole book.

Suppose you've opened 5,000 chests and found no Vehicle. The odds were 1 in 7,813. Are you "getting close"? Are you due?

No. Your next chest has exactly the same chance as your very first one: 1 in 7,813. The game isn't keeping score. It doesn't know you've been unlucky, and it wouldn't care if it did. Every chest is a brand-new roll of the dice. Epic said so in plain words: the rates are constant.

Believing you're "due" has a name: the gambler's fallacy. It's one of the most famous mistakes in all of human thinking, and it has a famous story.

True story: the night black came up 26 times

On 18 August 1913, at a casino in Monte Carlo, the little ball on one roulette wheel landed on black. Then black again. And again. After about fifteen blacks in a row, a crowd had gathered, and they all had the same thought: red must be due. They bet on red. Black. They doubled their bets. Black. They doubled again.

The ball landed on black 26 times in a row, and the gamblers lost a fortune, because the wheel had no idea what it had done on the previous spin. Every spin was a fresh start. The chance of 26 blacks in a row on that kind of wheel is about 1 in 137 million. But the chance of black on spin 27 was exactly what it always was: a bit under half.

Does that mean the past is useless? Not quite. Some things do have memory. If you draw cards from a pack without putting them back, the pack changes: once all four aces are out, there are no more aces. But a chest is like a coin, not a pack of cards. It's put back and shuffled every single time.

Work it out: You flip a fair coin and get heads five times in a row. What's the chance that the sixth flip is heads?

Answer: 1 in 2. The coin doesn't remember. (Five heads in a row was unlikely before you started, 1 in 32. But it's already happened. The sixth flip is a new flip.)

Detective work: reading a Gizmo pile

Averages may not be promises, but they're brilliant for making an estimate. Remember the hunter in Chapter 3 with 19, 15, 42, 84 and 26 Gizmos? Add them up: 186 Gizmos.

Gizmos drop about once in every 127 chests. So to collect 186 of them, that player has probably opened around 186 × 127 = 23,600 chests. At 200 an hour, that's nearly 120 hours of hunting, in the first week or so of the season.

We can't be exact: they might have been a bit lucky or a bit unlucky, and they might have used some Gizmos up. But with that many drops, the luck mostly evens out, and the estimate will be close. That's another rule worth knowing: averages can't tell you what will happen next, but they're very good at describing what happened over a long time.

And here's the sad bit. In 23,600 chests, what was that player's chance of at least one Pirate Shop Access? Use the trick: about 16%. Five times out of six, a week of heroic hunting ends with no token. Which is exactly what happened.

So treasures are rare, and nobody's ever due. Then how on earth did one squad find six of them in a single match?
