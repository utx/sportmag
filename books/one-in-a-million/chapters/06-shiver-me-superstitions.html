Chapter 6
Shiver me superstitions

Sailors have always been a superstitious lot. Real pirates and seafarers believed that whistling on deck would summon a storm, that setting sail on a Friday was asking for trouble, and that a cat on board brought good luck. (That last one may even have worked, though not by magic: cats eat the rats that eat the ship's food.)

It makes sense. The sea is dangerous and unpredictable, and when your life depends on luck, you'll try anything that might nudge it your way.

The treasure hunters of Crossbones Isle turned out to be just the same. Within days of the season starting, the hunt had its own set of superstitions. Let's put three of them to the test.

Superstition 1: "The tables are hot!"

When the squad in Chapter 5 found three treasures early in their match, they decided the match itself was lucky: the tables were "hot", the game was "boosted", and more treasure was sure to follow.

It's a lovely feeling. But we know from Chapter 4 that chests have no memory. A match that has already given you three treasures is exactly as likely to give you another as a match that's given you none. The fourth, fifth and sixth banners weren't caused by the first three. They were just more rolls of the same dice.

So why does it feel as though luck comes in streaks? Because real randomness is much lumpier than people expect.

True story: the bombs that weren't aimed

In 1944, during the Second World War, flying bombs fell on London. Londoners noticed that some neighbourhoods were hit again and again while others escaped, and a rumour spread that the bombs were being carefully aimed at particular streets.

After the war, a statistician called R. D. Clarke checked. Clarke divided a map of south London into 576 equal squares and counted the hits in each one. Then he compared the pattern with what pure chance would produce. They matched almost perfectly. The bombs had fallen at random. The clusters that looked like a plan were exactly the clusters that chance creates on its own.

Random doesn't mean "evenly spread". Scatter a handful of rice on the floor and you'll see clumps and bare patches, not a neat grid. In the same way, treasures arrive in bunches and droughts. Our brains, which are superb at spotting patterns, look at a bunch and shout "hot streak!" That's called the clustering illusion.

Word Watch

Superstitionnoun
A belief that an action or object brings good or bad luck, even though there's no real connection between them.
Illusionnoun
Something that seems real or true but isn't. An optical illusion tricks your eyes; the clustering illusion tricks your sense of chance.
Ritualnoun
A set of actions done the same way every time, often because people believe it matters.
Evidencenoun
Facts that help show whether something is true. Good thinkers ask for evidence before they believe a claim.
Conspiracy theorynoun
A belief that a secret group is hiding the truth and controlling events, usually held without good evidence.

Superstition 2: "Pretend you don't care"

Later in that same match, the squad came up with a plan. The treasure, they joked, only comes to people who aren't trying. So they would act as though they didn't want it. Be nonchalant. "I don't even want a Gizmo. I just want to play this game normally."

They were having fun, and they knew it. One of them compared it to being a kid and pretending not to care about a slow-loading game "so it would load faster". But lots of people half-believe things like this, and there's a reason.

Think about who notices what. If you're desperately hunting, every empty chest stings, and you remember hundreds of them. If your friend is playing normally and a treasure pops up, it's a wonderful surprise, and it's all anyone talks about. So it seems as though the people who don't care get the treasure. Really, they just open chests without counting the misses.

There are other rituals too. Early in the season, some players insisted you must smash each chest after opening it. Others swore you must never smash it. Neither does anything. (One of the hunters in Chapter 5 joked that they might have started that rumour themselves, just to slow everybody else down.)

True story: the superstitious pigeons

In 1948, an American scientist called B. F. Skinner put hungry pigeons in a box where a machine delivered a bit of food at regular intervals, no matter what the bird was doing. The pigeons didn't need to do anything at all.

But they didn't know that. Whatever a pigeon happened to be doing when the food arrived, it did more of. One bird began turning in circles. Another kept poking its head into a corner. Another swung its head like a pendulum. Each had "worked out" a ritual that made the food appear, and kept at it, even though the food would have come anyway.

Skinner called the report "Superstition" in the Pigeon. We're cleverer than pigeons. But when we do a lucky spin before a chest, we're doing the pigeon dance.

Superstition 3: "It's rigged!"

This is the one that stopped being funny.

In the first week, when hardly anyone had found the token, some players decided there must be a secret. Only a few tokens were "switched on" each day. Leakers knew the real odds and were hiding them. Epic's staff were quietly handing tokens to famous players. That kind of belief, a hidden plot instead of plain bad luck, is called a conspiracy theory.

Now that we know the odds, we can see what really happened. At 1 in 137,500, a token is simply very rare. And when famous players did find treasures, there's a simple reason: they open far more chests than anybody else, for hours every day, with thousands of people watching. More chests, more treasure. No plot needed.

How can you tell a good explanation from a conspiracy theory? Ask three questions.

Before you believe it, ask...
1
WHAT'S THE EVIDENCE?
"I feel like it's rigged" is a feeling. Numbers are evidence.
2
COULD CHANCE EXPLAIN IT?
With millions of tries, rare things happen every day.
3
WHO KEEPS THE SECRET?
The more people a secret plot needs, the less likely it is.

Be angry at the odds, never at the people

Here's the serious part. During that first week, some players sent hateful messages to the people who work on Fortnite. Some said staff should lose their jobs. A few said far worse things.

That is never all right. Not when you're frustrated, not when you've opened ten thousand chests, not ever. The people who make games are people. They have families, and they read what's written about them.

It's completely fair to think the odds are too stingy. Plenty of players said so politely, and it worked: Epic published the numbers and said the boosted rates might go up. That's how you change things. You say what's wrong, clearly, with evidence, and without being cruel.

If you ever see someone being nasty like that online, don't join in, and tell a grown-up if it worries you.

Try it: Next time you or a friend says "that always happens!" or "that never happens!", stop and count. How many times has it really happened, out of how many tries? You'll be surprised how often "always" turns out to mean "twice".
