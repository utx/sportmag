Chapter 2
One in what?

Flip a coin. It lands heads or tails. Two things can happen, and they're equally likely, so the chance of heads is 1 in 2.

Roll a dice. Six things can happen. The chance of a six is 1 in 6.

Open a chest on Crossbones Isle. The chance of a Gizmo is 1 in 127. Imagine a dice with 127 sides, with a tiny picture of a Gizmo on just one of them. Every chest rolls that dice once.

That's all odds are. "1 in something" means: picture that many equally likely outcomes, and only one of them is the win.

Three ways to say the same thing

Mathematicians have three ways of writing a chance, and it's worth being able to hop between them.

As odds: 1 in 4.

As a fraction: ¼ (one quarter).

As a percentage: 25%. "Per cent" is Latin for "out of a hundred", so 25% means 25 out of every 100.

To turn "1 in something" into a percentage, do 100 ÷ the something. So 1 in 4 is 100 ÷ 4 = 25%. And a Gizmo, at 1 in 127, is 100 ÷ 127 = about 0.79%. That's less than one per cent: fewer than one chest in every hundred.

Word Watch

Outcomenoun
One of the things that can happen. A coin flip has two outcomes; a dice roll has six.
Per centnoun
Out of every hundred. From the Latin per centum. The same "cent" as in century (100 years) and centimetre (a hundredth of a metre).
Independentadjective
In probability, two events are independent when one has no effect on the other. Your second coin flip doesn't know what your first one did.
Consecutiveadjective
One straight after another, with no gaps. Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday are consecutive days.

The heads-in-a-row trick

Here's a way to feel big odds in your bones. All you need is a coin and one rule:

To find the chance of two independent things both happening, multiply their chances.

One head: 1 in 2. Two heads in a row: 1 in 2 × 2 = 1 in 4. Three heads in a row: 1 in 2 × 2 × 2 = 1 in 8. Every extra head doubles the number.

Doubling doesn't sound like much. But keep going, and it runs away from you faster than a Shockwave.

Now the treasures make sense. Finding a Gizmo in a chest is like flipping seven heads in a row. Unlikely, but if you tried a few hundred times you'd expect to manage it.

Finding Pirate Shop Access in a chest is like flipping seventeen consecutive heads. Try it. You'll get three in a row fairly soon. Five in a row will take a while. Seventeen? You could flip coins every evening for years and never see it.

And the Gold Outfit sits between twenty and twenty-one heads in a row. Every chest is a fresh attempt at flipping twenty heads, one after another, without a single tail.

Work it out: What are the odds of rolling a six on a dice three times in a row?

Answer: 6 × 6 × 6 = 216, so 1 in 216. That's a little likelier than finding a Loading Screen (1 in 272) in one chest.

Rice on a chessboard

There's an old story from India about this kind of doubling. A wise man invents the game of chess, and the delighted king offers him any reward he likes. The wise man asks for rice: one grain on the first square of the chessboard, two on the second, four on the third, eight on the fourth, doubling each time until all 64 squares are covered.

The king laughs at such a small request. Then his treasurers start counting. By square 21, they need more than a million grains. By square 41, more than a million million. To fill the whole board they would need hundreds of years' worth of all the rice the entire world grows today. The story is a legend, but the maths is real, and it has a name: exponential growth.

It's the same reason Pirate Shop Access is so hard to find. Seventeen doesn't sound like many heads. But seventeen doublings turn 1 in 2 into 1 in 131,072.

Picture it in real life

Here's one more way to picture the two rarest treasures.

Stadium Australia, at Sydney Olympic Park, holds about 83,500 people when it's full. Imagine a full house for a big final, and then fill most of a second stadium as well, until you have 137,500 people. One of them, just one, has a golden ticket under their seat. Your chance of sitting in that seat is the chance of Pirate Shop Access in any single chest.

For the Gold Outfit, you'd need nearly nineteen full stadiums. One seat. One ticket.

Work it out: A full pack of playing cards has 52 cards. What are the odds of pulling out the ace of spades, putting it back, shuffling, and pulling it out again?

Answer: 52 × 52 = 2,704, so 1 in 2,704. That's almost three times likelier than finding the golden car in one chest (1 in 7,813).

But hold on. Nobody opens just one chest. A keen player might open two hundred in an hour. Surely that changes things? It does. And working out exactly how much is the best trick in this book.
