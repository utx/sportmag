Chapter 10
The treasure lab

A good scientist never takes anybody's word for it, including ours. Everything in this book can be tested at your kitchen table. You'll need a coin, two dice, a pencil, some paper and, for one experiment, a friend.

Each experiment starts with a prediction. Write yours down before you start. That's the rule that separates an experiment from just mucking about.

Word Watch

What you expect to happen, said before it happens. A prediction you can check is the heart of every experiment.

One go at an experiment. Flipping a coin once is one trial; scientists like lots of trials.
Samplenoun
The set of results you've collected. A bigger sample gives you a more trustworthy answer.
Simulateverb
To copy how something works using a simpler model, so you can study it. Seven coin flips can simulate one treasure chest.

Experiment 1: The seven-heads chest

The idea. In Chapter 4 we said a Gizmo (1 in 127) is about as likely as seven heads in a row (1 in 128). So seven coin flips can simulate one chest.

What to do. Flip a coin until you get a tail, then stop and write down how many heads you got first. That's one "chest". If you ever reach seven heads, shout "GIZMO!" Do this 30 times.

Prediction. How many of your 30 chests will contain a Gizmo?

What you'll probably find. None. Most chests end after zero, one or two heads. You'll be pleased with four. With 30 chests, your chance of at least one Gizmo is only about 21%. Now imagine needing seventeen.

Speed tip. You don't need to finish a chest. The moment you flip a tail, that chest is empty. Half your chests will be over after a single flip. That's what opening chests on the Isle is really like.

Experiment 2: The Chevalier's bet

The idea. Test the 370-year-old bet from Chapter 5: at least one six in four rolls of a dice. The maths says you should win 51.8% of the time.

What to do. Roll a dice four times. If any of them is a six, that's a win. Make a tally chart with two columns, WIN and LOSE, and play 40 rounds. (If you have four dice, roll them all at once. It's the same thing, and much quicker.)

Prediction. Out of 40 rounds, how many wins?

What you'll probably find. Somewhere around 21 wins, but don't be surprised by anything from 15 to 27. That's the lesson: 40 trials is a small sample, and small samples wobble. The Chevalier only noticed tiny differences because he played thousands of times. If you and a few friends all do this and add your results together, your combined answer will be much closer to 51.8%.

Experiment 3: Spot the fake

The idea. Chapter 3 said real randomness is lumpier than people expect. This experiment proves it, and it works like a magic trick.

What to do. You need a friend or a grown-up. Leave the room. While you're gone, they write down two lists of 50 Hs and Ts. For one list, they really flip a coin 50 times. For the other, they make it up, trying to look as random as possible. They don't tell you which is which.

Come back, look at both lists, and find the longest streak in each (the most Hs or Ts in a row).

How to spot the fake. The real list will almost always have a streak of five or more. The fake one almost never will, because when people invent "random" flips, they switch back and forth too often. Four heads in a row feels wrong to them, so they slip in a tail. The list with the long streak is the real one.

This is exactly why treasure seems to come in hot streaks and cold droughts. It isn't the game. It's what random looks like.

Experiment 4: Your own drop-rate log

The idea. Epic says the chance of any treasure is about 1 chest in 62. Check it.

What to do. Next time you play, keep a tally: one mark for every chest you open, and a star for every golden treasure banner. Bundle the marks in fives so they're easy to count. Keep the same sheet going across several days until you've logged at least 200 chests. (If you already own all of this week's weekly treasures, only Gizmos can drop, so your rate will be nearer 1 in 127.)

Experiment 5: Rice on a chessboard

The idea. Feel exponential growth with your own fingers.

What to do. Get a chessboard or draw an 8 × 8 grid, and a cup of uncooked rice (ask first). Put 1 grain on the first square, 2 on the second, 4 on the third, 8 on the fourth, and keep doubling.

Prediction. Which square will you reach before the rice won't fit on the square any more? Before the cup runs out?

What you'll probably find. Square 8 needs 128 grains, already a small heap. Square 11 needs 1,024. A whole cup of rice holds only around 8,000 grains, so you'll run dry on square 13 or 14, with fifty squares still to go. That's the top row and a half of the board. Square 18, the one that stands for Pirate Shop Access, would need about sixteen more cups.

The lab rules

  • Predict first. Write it down before you start.
  • Count everything. The misses matter as much as the hits.
  • More trials, more trust. One result proves nothing. A hundred start to mean something.
  • Be honest. If the result surprises you, that's the interesting bit. Don't nudge it.
