114

An independent commission has found Manchester City guilty of 114 of the 115 charges against them. Football is stunned. So what should happen next?

Investigation · The Touchline desk

For years, Manchester City's supporters had a reply ready whenever anyone mentioned the charges. Nothing has been proven. That reply no longer works. In late September, an independent commission, a sort of football court made up of legal experts, found City guilty of 114 of the 115 charges brought against them by the Premier League. It is one of the biggest scandals in the history of English football, and it has left the sport asking an uncomfortable question: how much of what City won was won fairly?

The basicsWhat were City accused of?

Football clubs in England have to follow rules about money. They must tell the Premier League the truth about how much they earn, from sponsors for example, and how much they pay their players and managers. There are also limits on how much money a club can lose. The point of the rules is simple: to stop one club using unlimited money to buy success while everyone else plays fair.

In February 2023, after an investigation lasting around four years, the Premier League charged City with 115 breaches. Most of them related to nine seasons, from 2009–10 to 2017–18. The charges fell into five groups:

  • 54 for not giving accurate financial information, including about where their money came from;
  • 14 for not giving full details of what they paid players and managers;
  • 5 for breaking UEFA's "financial fair play" rules;
  • 7 for breaking the Premier League's own spending rules;
  • 35 for failing to cooperate with the investigation.

The commission found City guilty of every charge in the first four groups, and of most of the charges for not cooperating. Richard Masters, the Premier League's chief executive, said the decision showed how the club had systematically broken the rules for nearly a decade.

Think of it like this

Imagine a game of Monopoly where every player gets the same money from the bank. One player keeps sneaking extra notes under the board, buys all the best streets, and wins easily. When the other players ask to check, that player won't show them the box. That, in very simple terms, is what City have been found guilty of. In football, that kind of behaviour brings the game into disrepute.

Hang on a minuteThe title United lost on goal difference

Here is the part that makes Manchester United supporters grind their teeth. On the final day of the 2011–12 season, United and City finished level on 89 points. City won the title on goal difference, thanks to Sergio Agüero's famous goal in the 94th minute against QPR. That title was won during the very years in which City have now been found guilty of breaking the financial rules. You can understand why some people at Old Trafford are looking at that trophy and wondering whether it really belongs in the other half of Manchester.

City's position is that they have done nothing wrong and that the appeal will clear them. They are entitled to make that case, and the appeal will be heard properly. But it's worth being clear about where things stand: an independent panel of experts examined the evidence for years and decided, on charge after charge, that City broke the rules. That is not the same as rival fans being jealous.

What happens nextAppeal, then punishment

Two things now happen at the same time. City's appeal will be heard by a separate panel, which must give its decision within 30 days of the hearing ending. Meanwhile, a different hearing will decide the sanction: the punishment. Reports suggest the whole process could be finished by February. There is no further appeal to the Court of Arbitration for Sport this time.

How big might the punishment be? There is a useful comparison. In 2023–24, Everton were docked a total of eight points and Nottingham Forest four for breaking the spending rules, and their breaches were far smaller. City have been found guilty of 114 breaches across nine seasons. Reports have suggested anything from an enormous fine (some say as much as £500 million) to a points deduction so large it would send City down. An unprecedented case may need an unprecedented punishment.

The Touchline tribunalHow should City be punished?

If we were in charge (sadly, nobody has asked us), these are the options we would consider. Each one gets a rating on our special Punish-o-Meter.

A giant fine

Make City pay a record amount of money. The problem: City's owners are among the richest people on Earth. A fine, even a huge one, might not hurt much.

Punish-o-Meter: ■■□□□ Not enough on its own

Strip the titles

Take away the Premier League titles won during the charged years: 2011–12, 2013–14 and 2017–18. And for 2011–12, the trophy could go to the team that finished level on points. We couldn't possibly say who that was.

Punish-o-Meter: ■■■■□ Fair, and it rights a wrong

A massive points deduction

Take away enough points to send City down to the Championship. It would be dramatic and painful, and it would show every club that cheating does not pay.

Punish-o-Meter: ■■■■■ The strongest message

A transfer ban

Stop City signing new players for two transfer windows. It would hit them where their advantage came from: the ability to buy almost anyone.

Punish-o-Meter: ■■■□□ A good extra

Our verdict

All of the above. Rules only mean something if breaking them has consequences. A points deduction big enough to relegate them, the titles from the charged seasons removed, plus a fine and a transfer ban. City should start again in the Championship and earn their way back, honestly. The integrity of the whole competition depends on it.

Of course, City still have the right to appeal, and that process must be fair. If the verdict stands, though, we can't see how anything less would do.
